# Catching Coding Errors Before Claims Ship

A pre-bill AI chart reviewer is a software checkpoint placed in the one-to-three-day gap between a clinician signing a visit note and the practice sending the insurance claim. For every visit it pulls the signed note and the proposed billing codes out of the practice's electronic health record (EHR). It tests whether the documentation supports each code under American Medical Association (AMA) and Medicare rules, then returns evidence-cited corrections to a biller, coder or clinician, who approves them before the claim leaves. The problem is large and measurable. Medicare's own auditors, in their 2025 report, found payment errors on **17.5% of sampled established-patient office-visit claim lines**, most of them visits billed at a higher level than the note supports ([CMS CERT 2025](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf)). Practices also leave money unbilled: the G2211 add-on code, worth **$17.37 per visit** in 2026, appears on only **14.5% of eligible Medicare visits** ([JAMA 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12921562/)). In this report's worked example, a bronchitis visit where the doctor also raised a blood-pressure dose was billed as a 99213 ($95.19 at 2026 Medicare national rates) but supports a 99214 plus G2211 ($152.98), a **$57.79 gap**. The same engine must just as readily cut an unsupported 99215 down to 99214 (−$56.78). The hard parts are not the language model. Billing codes and write-back sit outside the free federal data APIs and behind EHR partner contracts. Human coders agree on visit levels only **44–70%** of the time, so the "right answer" used for training and testing is itself noisy. Insurers now run their own downcoding algorithms, and the False Claims Act makes a revenue-only "upcoding engine" a legal hazard. We recommend an MVP aimed at independent primary care groups and groups run by management services organizations (MSOs) or private-equity (PE) platforms. It would integrate first with athenahealth, whose sandbox and partner APIs document reading notes, reading pending charges and writing charges, while running read-only audits on eClinicalWorks in parallel. The MVP would run six checks that correct codes in both directions, in suggest-only mode, priced at roughly **$0.75–$1.50 per chart reviewed** rather than as a share of "found" revenue. It needs roughly **9–12 months and $0.4–0.9M** to reach first production customers. The existential risk is AI coding built into the EHR itself (athenahealth Express Coding, Epic Penny). That is why the product should be positioned as an independent compliance reviewer that corrects in both directions, not as a revenue maximizer.

## A visit turns into a payment through a dozen hand-offs

**Outpatient** care is care where the patient goes home the same day: a clinic visit, an urgent care visit or a telehealth call. The bill for a clinician's own work is a **professional-fee claim**. That is the bill a physician, a nurse practitioner (NP, an advanced-practice nurse licensed to diagnose and prescribe) or a physician assistant (PA, a licensed clinician who practices medicine on a team) sends for their services. It travels on a different form, and through different Medicare accounting, from a hospital's "facility" or "institutional" claim ([CMS CERT 2025](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf)). The organization that pays is the **payer**. It can be traditional Medicare (the federal program for people 65 and older and some disabled people), which pays **fee-for-service** (a separate payment for each service), or **Medicare Advantage** (private plans that Medicare pays a fixed amount per enrollee and that in turn pay providers). It can also be Medicaid (the joint federal-state program for low-income people) or a **commercial insurer** such as UnitedHealthcare, Aetna or Cigna. The table follows one visit through the chain. The right-hand column marks where a pre-bill reviewer acts.

| Step | What happens | Who / which system | Where pre-bill review touches it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Schedule and register | Front desk records the patient's demographics and insurance and checks eligibility | **Practice-management (PM) system**, the scheduling and billing software that often comes bundled with the EHR | Reads the payer to pick payer-specific rules. In an Experian survey, 32% of providers named registration errors as a top denial cause ([Experian 2025](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2025/experian-health-s-3rd-annual-state-of-claims-survey-finds-denial)) |
| 2. See the patient and write the note | Clinician takes a history, examines, orders tests, decides a plan and writes the **note**, the narrative record of the visit | **EHR (electronic health record)**, the clinical charting software | The note is the primary evidence |

| 4. Sign the note; the charge "drops" | Signing locks the note. The EHR sends **charges** (billable lines) to the PM system | EHR to PM | **Trigger**: the reviewer detects the newly signed encounter |

The window in which a reviewer can act is short. MGMA, the medical group managers' association, reported a **median of about 3 days** from date of service to claim submission (2019 data) **[Survey]** ([MGMA 2019](https://www.mgma.com/getkaiasset/6a638b39-cce7-4b9a-83a9-04aca5a1c9f3/12.09.2019_Letter-Opposing-Section-305-FINAL.pdf)). In a 2020 pediatric benchmark, 63.8% of claims went out within 3 days, and vendors target charge entry within 24 hours **[Vendor]** ([PCC](https://chipsblog.pcc.com/claim-submission-delay-benchmark)). A product that takes longer than about one business day lengthens the time it takes the practice to get paid **[Inference]**. In most practices the person choosing the code is the clinician, not a professional coder. In HHS-OIG's study of 2011 data, most Medicare physicians assigned their own visit codes, and none of those with EHRs used the EHR's automatic coding module **[Authoritative, dated]** ([HHS-OIG 2012](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2012/use-of-electronic-health-record-systems-in-2011-among-medicare-physicians-providing-evaluation-and-management-services)). The 2021 AMA rules made clinician self-selection the default design by letting the clinician pick the level from decision-making or time ([AMA 2021](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2020-04/e-m-office-visit-changes.pdf)).

Almost nobody checks those choices before the bill goes out. In an April 2022 MGMA poll, 47% of groups audited internally, 34% used internal and external audits, 13% external only, and **6% did not audit at all** **[Survey]** ([MGMA 2022](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stats/the-most-asked-questions-about-medical-coding-audits-following-the-2021-e-m-and-mdm-changes)). The common audit baseline is about **10 charts per provider every 6–12 months** ([AAPC](https://www.aapc.com/codes/coding-newsletters/my-part-b-coding-alert/audits-dont-start-auditing-your-em-visits-without-a-baseline-article)). For a clinician seeing roughly 4,000 visits a year, that is about 0.25% coverage, and the audit happens after payment **[Inference]**. Scrubbers do not close the gap. They check the *claim* for format, code pairs and units, but they never read the *note*, so they cannot tell whether the documentation supports the level, add-on or modifier billed. That documentation-to-code gap is exactly what Medicare auditors and payer audits penalize, and it is the gap a pre-bill reviewer fills.

### Each claim line says what was done, why, where and for how much

A professional claim is a set of **service lines**, each built from a few small fields. The **CPT code** (Current Procedural Terminology, five characters, owned and licensed by the AMA) says *what* was done: 99214, for example, is an established-patient office visit of moderate complexity. **HCPCS Level II** codes (a letter plus four digits, published free by CMS) cover Medicare-specific services such as **G2211** and **G2212**, plus supplies and drugs ([CMS HCPCS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coding/hcpcsreleasecodesets/hcpcs-quarterly-update)). The **ICD-10-CM** code says *why*: it names the diagnosis. The set has about 72,000 billable "leaf" codes, and only the most specific code in each branch may be billed ([Yuan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07629)). A **modifier** is a two-character suffix that changes how a line is paid. Modifier 25, for example, says a visit was significant and separately identifiable from a procedure done the same day, and modifiers 95 and 93 mark video and audio-only telehealth. **Units** count repetitions. **Place of service (POS)** says where care happened: POS 11 is an office, POS 20 an urgent care facility, POS 02 and 10 telehealth. Each line points to up to four of the claim's diagnoses (the claim holds up to twelve), and the claim names the rendering clinician by their National Provider Identifier.

Medicare's price for each line comes from the **Physician Fee Schedule (PFS)**. Every code carries **relative value units (RVUs)**, a measure of physician work, practice expense and malpractice cost. Medicare multiplies the RVUs by a dollar **conversion factor**: **$33.4009** in 2026 for most clinicians, and $33.5675 for clinicians in qualifying advanced alternative payment models **[Authoritative]** ([CMS RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/payment/fee-schedules/physician/pfs-relative-value-files/rvu26d); [ACC](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2025/10/31/21/33/cms-releases-2026)). The national amount is then adjusted for local costs, split between Medicare and the patient's coinsurance, and reduced by a 2% budget "sequester" on Medicare's share. "Non-facility" rates apply in a private office. Lower "facility" rates apply when the clinic is a hospital outpatient department, where the hospital also bills its own facility fee. Commercial payers negotiate their own fee schedules, which are not public. A product can only show exact dollars if the customer loads its contracted rates, so Medicare's schedule is the default yardstick **[Inference]**. The 2026 values below are total RVUs multiplied by the conversion factor from CMS's October 2026 file **[Derived]** ([CMS RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/files/zip/rvu26d-updated-08-26-2026.zip)).

| 99213 | Established patient, low complexity | $95.19 | $57.45 | +$35.74 |

Each step up the ladder is worth **30–40% of the base visit payment**, so a one-level error in either direction is a large fraction of what the visit earns **[Inference]**.

### The visit level is a judgment scored on three axes

Office visits belong to the **evaluation and management (E/M)** code family: 99202–99205 for new patients and 99211–99215 for established ones. CPT treats a patient as established if they received professional services from the same group and specialty within the past three years. Since 2021 the clinician may pick the level by **medical decision making (MDM)** or by **total time** spent on the date of the visit. History and physical exam no longer set the level ([AMA 2021](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2020-04/e-m-office-visit-changes.pdf)). MDM has three elements: the number and complexity of **problems addressed**, the amount and complexity of **data** reviewed and analyzed, and the **risk** of the patient's management. "To qualify for a particular level of MDM, two of the three elements for that level of MDM must be met or exceeded" **[Authoritative]** ([AMA CPT E/M guidelines 2023](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf)). A condensed version of the AMA grid follows. The full table and definitions are in the AMA guideline document.

| MDM level (code) | Problems addressed | Data reviewed and analyzed | Risk of patient management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straightforward (99202/99212) | One self-limited or minor problem | Minimal or none | Minimal |
| Low (99203/99213) | Two or more minor problems; **or** one stable chronic illness; **or** one acute, uncomplicated illness or injury | **Limited**: any two of (review of each external note, review of each unique test result, ordering of each unique test), **or** an independent historian | Low (e.g., over-the-counter drugs) |
| Moderate (99204/99214) | One or more chronic illnesses with exacerbation, progression or side effects of treatment; **or** two or more stable chronic illnesses; **or** an undiagnosed new problem with uncertain prognosis; **or** an acute illness with systemic symptoms; **or** an acute complicated injury | **Moderate**: one of three categories (any three test/note/historian items; independent interpretation of a test; or discussion of management with an external clinician) | Moderate (e.g., **prescription drug management**, decision about minor surgery with risk factors, care limited by social determinants of health) |
| High (99205/99215) | Chronic illness with severe exacerbation; **or** an illness posing a threat to life or bodily function | **Extensive**: two of the three categories | High (e.g., drug therapy needing intensive toxicity monitoring, decision about hospitalization or emergency surgery) |

The time pathway uses minimum thresholds that "must be met or exceeded": **99212 10 minutes, 99213 20, 99214 30, 99215 40**, and 15/30/45/60 minutes for 99202–99205. Time counts the clinician's own work on that date, such as reviewing tests, examining, counseling, ordering and documenting. It excludes separately billed services and clinical staff time, and it must be documented when used ([AMA 2023](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf); [CMS RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/files/zip/rvu26d-updated-08-26-2026.zip)). Three details matter for software. First, a problem counts only if it was **addressed**, meaning evaluated or treated at this visit. A diagnosis that merely sits on the problem list does not count. Second, a patient "not at their treatment goal" is not stable, so an uncontrolled chronic condition scores as *moderate*. Third, a visit qualifies through *either* MDM or time, so the supported level is the higher of the two. In 2023 the same framework was extended to hospital, consultation, emergency, nursing-facility and home visits ([AMA 2023](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf)).

Add-on codes ride on top of the visit. **G2211** pays $17.37 when the clinician is the continuing focal point of the patient's care or manages a serious or complex condition over time. Medicare has paid it since 2024. **Prolonged services** differ by payer. CPT's 99417 starts at 55 minutes on a 99215. Medicare does not pay 99417; it pays **G2212**, which starts only at about **69 minutes** for an established patient and **89 minutes** for a new one ([CMS RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/files/zip/rvu26d-updated-08-26-2026.zip); [MedLearn](https://medlearn.com/icd10monitor/prolonged-services-in-cpt-versus-medicare-they-do-not-agree/)). The rules are also moving. CMS's **2027 proposed rule** (July 14, 2026, not final) would replace G2211 with a modifier that raises the visit payment by **16%**, or **32%** for clinicians in Medicare Shared Savings Program accountable care organizations (ACOs, groups that share savings and losses on a patient population). It would also pay a same-day visit plus a minor or major procedure at **50%** for the lower-valued service, and cut the conversion factor to **$32.84 (−1.68%)** **[Authoritative, proposed]** ([CMS CY2027 fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-proposed-rule)).

### Insurers check the claim first and the chart later

Before payment, claims run through deterministic edits. The **National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI)** publishes two kinds. **Procedure-to-procedure (PTP) edits** catch code pairs that should not be billed together without a modifier. **Medically unlikely edits (MUEs)** cap the units per line. CMS updates both quarterly ([CMS NCCI](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coding/ncci-coding-edits)). **Local and National Coverage Determinations (LCDs/NCDs)** state when Medicare considers a service "reasonable and necessary". Their billing articles list which diagnoses support which services ([CMS MLN](https://cms.gov/outreach-and-education/mln/educational-tools/mln901347-how-to-mcd/mcd/mcd/chapter_6_downloads)). Commercial payers layer their own reimbursement policies on top and publish them as PDFs, such as UnitedHealthcare's procedure-to-modifier policy ([UHC](https://www.uhcprovider.com/content/dam/provider/docs/public/policies/comm-reimbursement/COMM-Procedure-to-Modifier-Policy.pdf)). The payer then pays, partly pays, **denies** (refuses payment) or **downcodes** (pays a lower level than billed). Denials are rising. **41% of providers** report denial rates of 10% or more, up from 30% in 2022, and 90% of denials need human rework **[Survey]** ([Experian 2025](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2025/experian-health-s-3rd-annual-state-of-claims-survey-finds-denial)). About 60% of medical group leaders said denials rose in 2024 **[Survey]** ([MGMA Stat](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stat/strategic-improvements-in-your-rcm-to-reduce-your-practices-claim-denials)). In 2025, Medicare denied **2.7–5.2% of submitted office visit services**, depending on specialty **[Derived]** ([CMS PSPS 2025](https://data.cms.gov/data-api/v1/dataset/aa28896d-e502-4742-8b8d-65177ff45c7a/data)). Every denial costs money to work: hospitals spent an average of $43.84 per claim contesting private-payer denials in 2022 **[Survey]** ([Premier via Healthcare Finance News](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/private-payers-deny-15-claims-survey-finds)).

Commercial payers are now automating downcoding. **Cigna's policy R49** (effective October 1, 2025) lets Cigna reduce 99204–99205 and 99214–99215 by one level when the diagnoses and other claim data do not appear to support the complexity. Maryland fined Cigna $80,000 and ordered it to stop, and Cigna paused the policy for California HMO plans ([AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/fpm/blogs/getting-paid/cigna-downcoding-em); [WCHSB](https://insights.wchsb.com/2026/03/25/maryland-fines-cigna-80k-and-orders-it-to-stop-automatic-downcoding-what-every-provider-needs-to-know/); [CMA](https://www.cmadocs.org/newsroom/news/view/ArticleId/50993/Cigna-agrees-to-pause-controversial-downcoding-policy)). Aetna's Claim and Code Review Program targets level 4–5 visits in nearly all commercial states ([LUGPA](https://www.lugpa.org/insurer-downcoding-policies)). After payment, Medicare's **Comprehensive Error Rate Testing (CERT)** program samples paid claims, requests the medical records, and has reviewers judge whether each payment was correct. OIG runs targeted audits. Both measure exactly the documentation-to-code mismatch described above ([CMS CERT 2025](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf)).

## The reviewer works inside the window between signature and submission

Pre-bill AI chart review is software that does four jobs for every signed visit. It **fetches** the note and the pending codes. It **checks** whether the documentation supports each code, modifier, unit and diagnosis, and whether anything documented was left unbilled. It **routes** each finding to the right human. And it **writes** approved corrections back before the claim is built.

Charta Health is the reference product, and every account of its mechanics comes from Charta itself **[Vendor]**. Charta says that "every time a provider closes a note in your EHR, Charta identifies the encounter type and runs the analysis modules designed for that type of note." Its homepage screenshot shows a primary care note running "10 coding analyses and 8 clinical quality analyses." Charta lets each customer route errors to "direct autocorrect in your EHR, send automated notes to providers, or queue charts for human intervention" ([Charta homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/)). Federally qualified health center (FQHC) customers get results "within 24 hours of a patient note closing." A dashboard image showing "last sync at 12:01 AM, 7,847 charts pulled" suggests at least some deployments run nightly batches ([Charta FQHC page](https://chartahealth.com/for/fqhc); [Charta homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/)). Each deployment is configured over 2–8 weeks by a forward-deployed engineer who holds a Certified Professional Coder (CPC) credential, and it translates the customer's own audit checklist into checks ([Charta buyer's guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-chart-review-buyers-guide)). The sections below describe how a new entrant would build the same loop. The EHR and integration facts come from the research notes; the design choices are **[Recommendation]**.

### Notes and codes live in different places in every EHR

To decide anything, the reviewer needs six things for each visit. It needs the signed note text, and the context that drives the level: vitals, medication changes, orders and results, the problem list, and one or two prior visits. It needs the **pending charge lines**, meaning the CPT/HCPCS codes, modifiers, units, ICD-10 pointers, place of service and rendering clinician. And it needs the patient's age and sex, the payer, and the signer and signature time. HIPAA's "minimum necessary" rule applies directly to the vendor, so pulls should be scoped to the encounter and a short look-back, with the rationale documented ([45 CFR 164.502](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)).

The free federal route reads clinical data but not billing data. The 21st Century Cures Act made certified EHRs offer standardized **FHIR** APIs. FHIR is the modern web standard for exchanging health data as JSON over HTTPS, and the requirement sits in certification criterion **(g)(10)**. These APIs must expose the US Core data set (problems, medications, labs, encounters and at least eight clinical note types via the DocumentReference resource) for single patients and bulk groups. But they are **read-only by rule**: the criterion "specifically exclude[s] 'write' capabilities" **[Authoritative]** ([ONC (g)(10)](https://www.healthit.gov/test-method/standardized-api-patient-and-population-services); [US Core clinical notes](https://hl7.org/fhir/us/core/STU6.1/clinical-notes.html)). Claims and charges are not part of US Core. The closest a FHIR read gets to billing is the Encounter's `type` field, which *may* carry the E/M CPT code, plus "encounter-diagnosis" Condition resources ([US Core Encounter](https://hl7.org/fhir/us/core/STU6.1/StructureDefinition-us-core-encounter.html); [Encounter type value set](https://hl7.org/fhir/us/core/STU6.1/ValueSet-us-core-encounter-type.html)). Bulk export is built for hours-to-daily extracts, not same-day interception. A separate rule, (b)(10), requires a full export of a patient's electronic health information, including billing records, which is useful for backfilling historical charts but is a batch tool **[Inference]** ([ONC Developer RoundTable](https://www.healthit.gov/sites/default/files/2023-07/ONC_Developer_RoundTable_7-12-2023_508.pdf)).

The older workhorse is **HL7 version 2** messaging, a pipe-delimited standard carried by interface engines. Four message families matter. **ADT** messages announce visits and **ORU** messages carry results. **MDM** messages carry documents; this "MDM" means medical document management and is unrelated to medical decision making. **DFT** ("detail financial transaction", e.g., DFT^P03) messages carry charges with their procedure, diagnosis and modifier codes ([Nirmitee](https://nirmitee.io/blog/epic-bridges-integration/)). Each interface is built per customer, at an estimated $8,000–$15,000 per interface plus $3,000–$15,000 a year to maintain **[Vendor]** ([Taction](https://www.tactionsoft.com/ehr-integration-cost-guide/)). EHR vendors' own partner APIs are the third route, and they differ sharply.

Four other routes exist, and each is worse as a primary channel **[Inference]**. The first is **intercepting at the claim or clearinghouse stage**. The 837 claim already carries every code, and Stedi's pay-as-you-go API charges about $0.10–$0.30 per claim (cached pricing that may be stale) and offers a validation endpoint ([Stedi](https://www.stedi.com/pricing)). But this route loses the note, edits the claim out of sync with the PM record, depends on which clearinghouse the practice uses, and is blocked where the EHR runs its own clearinghouse, as athenaOne does ([athenahealth Claim Rules](https://help.athenahealth.com/Ohelp/Content/Features/Claim_Rules_A.htm)).

The second route is a **normalizing platform such as Redox**. These translate HL7 and FHIR across EHRs, including financial-transaction and notes models, for a median of about $52,500 a year (range $12,000–$148,000) ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/redox); [Redox](https://developer.redoxengine.com/data-models/)). That makes sense only once several health-system customers arrive. The third, **national-network record aggregators** (Particle, Metriport, Health Gorilla), return records shared for treatment purposes after the fact and cannot see a pending charge. The fourth is **robotic process automation (RPA)**: software that clicks through the EHR's web screens with a user's login takes about 2–4 weeks to build, but "most teams see it break within the same quarter" ([Integuru](https://www.integuru.com/blog/ehr-integration-healthcare-ai)). RPA also carries contract risk, so it is at most a temporary bridge for reads, never a way to write to the billing ledger.

### Checks run in layers: rules first, AI extraction second, verification last

Large language models (LLMs) are poor at recalling exact codes but good at pulling structured facts out of text. A product should therefore use the model to *read* the note and let deterministic rules *decide* the code **[Inference, grounded in the evidence below]**. In a NEJM AI study, GPT-4 reproduced exact codes from their official descriptions only **33.9% of the time for ICD-10-CM and 49.8% for CPT** ([Soroush et al. via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2024/04/240422120603.htm)). Oracle Health's ProFees system took a narrower approach. An LLM grades the three MDM elements, an LLM critic checks each one, and a rule-based decision tree applies the 2-of-3 rule. The system beat a commercial E/M coder by **36.85 points** in CPT accuracy **[Authoritative, peer-reviewed industry track]** ([ProFees, EMNLP 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25007v1)). The pipeline below is the concrete form of that design.

| Stage | What the software does | Technique | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Configure | Load customer payers, fee schedules, local policies and routing rules; pick the rule pack versions valid for the date of service | Versioned rules store keyed by date of service | Per-encounter rule context |
| 1. Trigger | Detect a signed encounter with new charges | athenaOne changed-data polling; Epic MDM message; FHIR polling by date on eCW | Encounter ID |
| 2. Fetch | Pull the note, vitals, medications, orders/results, problem list, one or two prior visits, pending charge lines, payer and signer | EHR API / FHIR / HL7 | Raw bundle |
| 3. Normalize | Split the note into sections (history, exam, assessment and plan); map charges to an internal schema; set new vs established and telehealth flags | Parsers | Canonical encounter |
| 4. Deterministic pre-checks | NCCI code-pair and unit limits; add-on code without its primary code; valid, billable ICD-10 leaf; age/sex edits; signature and date present; rendering clinician equals signer; POS and telehealth modifier consistency; payer recognition of codes such as G2211 versus G2212/99417 | Lookup tables from CMS files and payer rules | Hard errors (high certainty, near-zero cost) |
| 5. LLM extraction | Return JSON listing each problem and its status; each data item (tests ordered or reviewed, external notes, independent historian); each risk item (prescription changes, surgery decisions, social determinants); documented time; procedures and whether the visit is separately identifiable; diagnosis specificity cues. Every item carries a **quoted span** from the note | Structured output with fixed schema; smaller model | Evidence-linked facts |
| 6. Rules engine | Apply the MDM grid and time thresholds, take the higher supported level, and compare with the billed level; expand ICD-10 candidates to sibling codes and verify | Deterministic grid; "generate, expand, verify" for ICD-10 | Proposed level and code changes |
| 7. Verify discrepancies only | A stronger model, or two or more samples, re-reads only where billed and supported codes differ | Critic / self-consistency | Confirmed or dropped finding |
| 8. Score and price | Combine extraction agreement, rule certainty and dollar impact from the PFS or customer fee schedule | Scoring | Confidence and $ per finding |
| 9. Route | Coder queue (default); clinician query for documentation gaps; auto-apply only for opt-in deterministic fixes; suppress low-confidence findings | Policy | Work items |
| 10. Log | Store model, prompt and rule-file versions, evidence, approver and timestamp | Append-only store | Audit trail |

Running deterministic checks first and model verification only on discrepancies keeps cost proportional to findings. In ProFees, the critic added 4 points of CPT accuracy, while three-way self-consistency added only 1.2 points at **three times the cost** ([ProFees](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25007v1)). For ICD-10, giving a verifier the *descriptions* of sibling and cousin codes lifted a small fine-tuned model to 57.6 F1. The authors found that many errors are hierarchical near-misses, such as I11.0 versus I11.9 ([Yuan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07629)). Retrieving candidate codes before choosing let small open-source models approach large commercial ones on emergency-department diagnoses ([Klang et al. via AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/simple-retrieval-step-boosts-ai-accuracy-in-assigning-icd-codes)).

### Corrections flow back through a hold and a human approver

A pre-bill fix only works if the claim waits for it. athenaOne's rules engine can hold claims, post warnings or change format, and practices can add custom rules ([athenahealth Claim Rules](https://help.athenahealth.com/Ohelp/Content/Features/Claim_Rules_A.htm)). NextGen keeps EHR charges in a holding tank until someone accepts them. Epic sends signed-encounter charges to charge-review workqueues. A product should slot into these native holding points, reading at "note signed / charge created" and writing before "claim created or released" ([NextGen](https://docs.nextgen.com/en-US/help-guide-for-nextgenc2ae-enterprise-pm-8-3239701/process-nextgenc2ae-enterprise-ehr-charges-from-the-charge-posting-window-360694); [AAPC forum](https://forums.aapc.com/discuss/goto/post?id=471041)). Write-back takes a different form on each EHR. On athenaOne it is a proprietary charge or claim call after the biller approves; on Epic, an inbound DFT through Incoming Financial Transactions; on eClinicalWorks, a ChargeItem create under contract; and on ModMed, a task for a biller, because charges cannot be updated through the API. Wherever no write path exists yet, the product falls back to a worklist that a biller keys in by hand **[Inference]**.

Documentation gaps take a different route. The product sends a **non-leading query** to the clinician, who may add an addendum to the note only if the facts are true. Epic exposes a coding-query interface for exactly this ([open.epic Coding](https://open.epic.com/Financial/Coding)). The product itself never writes clinical text. Every finding, accepted or rejected, is logged with its evidence and versions so the practice can answer a payer audit years later. Acceptance and rejection decisions double as training and evaluation labels **[Recommendation]**.

## One bronchitis visit hides a $57.79 underbilling

The example below is illustrative. It uses real 2026 Medicare national prices and the AMA's MDM rules **[Derived from Authoritative]**. The patient is a 68-year-old established patient with traditional Medicare, seen in a primary care office (POS 11). The signed note reads, in condensed form:

> Chief complaint: cough and fever for five days. History: productive cough, temperature to 101°F at home, no shortness of breath. Vitals: temperature 100.4°F, blood pressure 158/96 (last visit 152/94). Exam: scattered rhonchi, no focal findings. In-office rapid influenza A/B antigen test: negative. Assessment and plan: (1) Acute bronchitis, likely viral: fluids, over-the-counter cough suppressant, return if worse. (2) Essential hypertension, not at goal on lisinopril 10 mg: increase to lisinopril 20 mg daily, home blood-pressure log, recheck in 4 weeks. Electronically signed by the treating physician on the date of service.

The physician picked 99213 and only the bronchitis diagnosis, so the pending claim looked like this:

| Line | Code | Modifier | Diagnosis pointer | 2026 Medicare national (office) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 99213, established visit, low MDM | — | A | $95.19 |
| 2 | 87804, rapid influenza antigen test | QW (see note below) | A | Paid from the separate Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule; unchanged by this review |
| Claim diagnoses | A = J20.9, acute bronchitis, unspecified | | | |

Medicare requires a **QW** modifier on certain lab tests performed with simple "CLIA-waived" kits. CLIA is the federal lab-quality law, and waived tests are simple enough to run in an office. That requirement is this report's own domain knowledge and is not covered by the research notes, so confirm it against CMS's current waived-test list.

The engine handles the visit in ten steps.

|---|---|---|
| 1. Trigger | athenaOne's changed-data feed reports a newly signed encounter with new claim charges | Encounter queued within minutes |
| 2. Fetch | Encounter summary (history, exam, assessment and plan), vitals, medication list (lisinopril), problem list (hypertension), the two pending lines and the diagnosis | Canonical encounter |
| 3. Classify | Established patient; in-person office visit; payer is Medicare fee-for-service, so the Medicare rule pack for the 2026 date of service applies | Rule context set |
| 4. The I10 fix has a second, less obvious value. Algorithms like Cigna R49 judge a 99214 from the *claim's* diagnoses. ## About one claim line in six is wrong, in both directions

Stated precisely, the problem is this **[Recommendation]**. For every outpatient professional claim, before submission, the software must decide whether the signed documentation supports each billed code, modifier, unit and diagnosis under the specific payer's rules, and find services the note supports but the claim omits. It must deliver an evidence-cited correction to the person accountable for the claim within the practice's one-to-three-day billing window. It must flag precisely enough that reviewers trust it and spend seconds, not minutes, per finding, at a cost far below manual review. And it must treat downward corrections with the same priority as upward ones, leaving an audit trail that defends both.

The evidence on how often each type of error occurs is uneven. Federal audits measure overbilling well, while underbilling shows up mainly in small studies.

The 7:1 ratio is easy to misread. CERT reviews only what was *billed*, so an add-on that was never billed can never appear as an underpayment. CERT therefore shows that billed claims mostly err high. It does not show that undercoding is rare **[Inference]**. The two error populations call for two jobs. *Compliance* means catching unsupported 99215/99205 visits, modifier 25, 99211 and missing signatures. *Revenue* means catching supported-but-unbilled levels and add-ons. A product doing only the second job looks like a one-way upcoding engine, the legal theory behind several large settlements discussed below. Older evidence points the same way. OIG's 2010-data review found **42% of E/M claims incorrectly coded**, mostly upcoded ([HHS-OIG 2014](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2014/improper-payments-for-evaluation-and-management-services-cost-medicare-billions-in-2010/)). A 2001 study found family physicians agreed with expert coders only about half the time, mostly undercoding established visits and overcoding new ones ([King et al. 2001](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/commhealth_fac/73/)).

Manual review is cheap per chart but covers almost nothing. Consultants quote **$20–$40 per chart** or $100–$200 an hour **[anecdotal]** ([AAPC forum](https://aapc.com/discuss/goto/post?id=27832)). Coders earn a median of **$50,250** (medical records specialists, May 2024; $45,620 in physician offices) **[Authoritative]** ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/medical-records-and-health-information-technicians.htm)). Holders of the CPC credential average **$64,495** (2023) **[Survey]** ([AAPC](https://aapc.com/resources/salary-by-credential)). Reviewing every chart by hand, even at $5 a chart, would cost about $20,000 per clinician per year, more than most leakage estimates recover. That cost gap is why "100% pre-bill review" was not feasible before AI **[Inference]**. Charta's investors describe the status quo as about 1–2% of charts reviewed, after billing ([Madrona](https://www.madrona.com/charting-healthcares-ai-transformation-investing-in-charta-health/)).

Two segment facts reshape the opportunity **[Inference]**. First, a **federally qualified health center (FQHC)**, a community health center funded to serve underserved patients, is paid a flat **prospective payment system (PPS)** rate per visit by Medicare and Medicaid. Moving a visit from 99213 to 99214 therefore usually changes nothing for those payers. Charta's FQHC case studies, such as "38% of E/M codes were simply underleveled", deliver mostly compliance and commercial-claim value, not visit-level revenue ([Charta neighborhood FQHC](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/audit-proof-compliance-for-neighborhood-fqhc)). Second, family practice and internal medicine already bill 99214 on about 63% of established visits. In primary care the money is less in lifting 99213s and more in add-ons, diagnosis linkage and defending the 99214s already billed.

A simple model shows the potential size **[Derived arithmetic on authoritative prices; assumptions are this report's]**. Take 1,000 established visits with the 2025 family-practice level mix (an average Medicare payment of about $124 a visit) and make three assumptions. If 10% of the 284 99213 visits actually support 99214, that adds about **$1,150**. If G2211 capture rises from 24% to 60% of eligible visits, that adds about **$6,200**. If 2% of visits qualify for G2212, that adds about **$680**. The total is roughly **$8,000, or 6–7% of visit revenue**. A truly two-directional product would also take back the roughly 3.1% of established-visit dollars that CERT finds upcoded, about $3,900, leaving a **net of roughly $4,200 (≈3.4%)**. The money taken back is not lost value: it avoids future denials, recoupments and audits. This model sits below Charta's claimed **11% average revenue uplift** ([Bain Capital Ventures](https://baincapitalventures.com/insight/charting-a-new-era-how-charta-health-is-transforming-medical-chart-review/)), which is **[Vendor]** and has no published sample, baseline or period. Its net figure also sits inside the vendor-sourced folklore that practices lose "3–5% of net revenue" to charge-capture failures ([CapMinds](https://www.capminds.com/blog/reducing-billing-errors-and-revenue-leakage-in-mid-sized-practices/)). G2211 dominates the model, so the figure shrinks sharply for practices with few Medicare patients.

### Seven reasons the problem resists software

**The rules are judgment calls, and experts disagree.** "Addressed", "not at goal", "prescription drug management" and "independent interpretation" all require clinical reading. In one widely cited benchmark, **136 certified coders** coding six notes agreed on the correct code only **58.7%** of the time ([Psychiatric News 2002](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/pn.37.7.0010)). In a 2000 pilot, five experienced coders unanimously agreed on only one of six notes ([JABFM 2000](https://www.jabfm.org/content/13/2/144.full.pdf)). In ProFees's development set, an expert agreed with the professional coder's E/M code on only **44%** of encounters ([ProFees](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25007v1)). No post-2021 agreement study was found. Any accuracy claim therefore needs an adjudicated gold standard, not a single coder's answer.

**Models cannot be trusted to remember codes.** Newer studies still report 40–82% exact-match CPT accuracy on specialty notes with no consistent winner among Claude 4.x, Gemini and GPT-5.x models ([Spine J 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1529943026006418); [PubMed 42169810](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42169810/); [Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-8475390/v1)). The 2024 baseline was 33.9–49.8%. Bundling logic is a weak spot, so table-driven decisions belong in code, not in prompts.

**Notes are long, templated and cloned.** Across about 3 million Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) outpatient notes, median length grew from 401 to **642 words** between 2009 and 2018. By 2018, **59%** of note text duplicated the patient's prior note and 71% came from templates or copy-paste ([OHSU, Rule et al.](https://digitalcollections.ohsu.edu/record/9251/files/Rule-Adam-OHSU-ResearchWeek-2021.pdf)). Copied text makes "listed" look like "addressed", the exact distinction that flips levels in the worked example.

**Data access is gated exactly where the product needs it.** Billing codes and write-back sit outside the free (g)(10) APIs. Production access runs through partner contracts and per-practice activation. On eClinicalWorks, polling is capped at 250 calls a minute per practice, all inside a roughly three-day billing window. Engineering is rarely the critical path. The critical path is a sponsoring design partner, the EHR's partner contract and security evidence **[Inference]** ([Sidebench](https://sidebench.com/ehr-integration-cost-bands-2026/)).

**Rules differ by payer and change constantly.** NCCI edits change quarterly, ICD-10-CM every October 1 (the fiscal-year 2027 set went live October 1, 2026), CPT every January and the PFS every year ([CDC ICD-10-CM](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/icd/comprehensive-listing-of-icd-10-cm-files.htm); [CMS NCCI](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coding/ncci-coding-edits)). Medicare's G2212 differs from CPT's 99417 in both code and threshold. G2211 is not universal among commercial payers and may become a modifier in 2027. Commercial policies arrive as unstructured PDFs.

**The legal environment punishes one-way products.** Insurers and regulators watch for coding intensity, meaning a rise in average billed levels that may not reflect sicker patients. A Blue Cross Blue Shield Association analysis attributed about **$942M** of 2023–2025 inpatient cost to coding changes. Trilliant Health found outpatient visit levels shifting upward at six large systems after they adopted AI scribes. A PwC survey found about 70% of health plans rank provider AI coding tools among their top three cost inflators ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/01/health-care-costs-insurance-claims-billing-ai.html); [Trilliant Health](https://www.trillianthealth.com/increased-outpatient-coding-intensity-following-hospital-adoption-of-ai-enabled-scribing); [STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2026/06/03/ai-arms-race-medical-building-waste/)). A customer whose level-4/5 share jumps after adoption invites downcoding and audits.

**Human time, not compute, sets the unit cost.** A full coder review costs about **$1.20–$7 per chart**, at $24–$40 an hour loaded and 6–20 charts an hour. A typical LLM pipeline costs cents to about $1 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [AAPC forum](https://www.aapc.com/discuss/threads/coding-productivity.155725); [UNM Health RFP](https://fr.unmhealth.org/about/_files/_files-current-bids/rfp-p427-20-medical-coders-and-auditors-5th-amendment-10-6-20.pdf)). Flag precision therefore drives both customer trust and margin **[Inference]**.

## The MVP: six checks in both directions for primary care

### Primary care groups on athenaOne are the wedge, eClinicalWorks the second front

**[Recommendation]** Target independent and MSO- or PE-backed primary care groups with roughly 10–150 clinicians. That includes NP- and PA-heavy practices and multi-site urgent-care-plus-primary-care operators *not* on Experity. The ideal group has a meaningful Medicare or Medicare Advantage share, fee-for-service-dominant payment, and a central billing team. Primary care has the most volume in the most standardized code set. One rule pack covers most of the dollars: 99202–99215 by MDM or time, G2211, G2212/99417, annual-wellness-plus-problem visits with modifier 25, and telehealth POS and modifiers. The value runs in both directions: G2211 at 24% capture on one side, internal medicine's 5.7% CERT rate and algorithmic downcoding on the other. Buyers are ROI-driven practice administrators, MSO revenue-cycle leaders and CFOs. Physician ownership is shrinking: physician-owned private practice fell to **42.2%** of physicians in 2024 while PE-owned practices rose to **6.5%**, so MSO and PE platforms aggregate many practices under one buyer ([Medical Economics on AMA benchmark](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/independent-practice-losing-ground-to-hospital-corporate-private-equity-ownership)). Outpatient AI purchases close in about **4.7 months** on average, against 6.6 for health systems **[Survey]** ([Menlo Ventures](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-ai-in-healthcare/)).

Start production integration on **athenaOne**. It is the only major ambulatory EHR whose self-serve sandbox and partner APIs document all three parts of the loop: reading notes, reading pending charges and writing charges. It has native claim holds and a Marketplace that reaches roughly 160,000 providers. It also leads KLAS rankings for exactly the 11–75-physician independent groups targeted ([KLAS via Healthcare IT News](https://www.healthcareitnews.com/news/best-klas-2026-sees-positive-disruption-tangible-tech-improvements)). The cost is competition. Charta joined the athenahealth Marketplace in June 2026, several smaller athena-integrated coding tools exist, and athenahealth's own Express Coding launched in summer 2026 ([Charta](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/charta-health-athenahealth-marketplace); [Ember](https://www.embercopilot.ai/knowledge/7-ai-coding-tools-that-seamlessly-integrate-with-athenahealth); [athenahealth](https://www.athenahealth.com/resources/blog/introducing-express-coding-athenaone)). Run **eClinicalWorks** as a second front from the first month. It has the larger installed base (11.9% vs. 6.9% of installs), free certified reads, and no dedicated native AI coding product found. Use free reads for paid retrospective audits and a biller worklist while negotiating its write contract ([eCW](https://www.eclinicalworks.com/resources/certified-ehr-technology/)). If an MSO design partner on eClinicalWorks signs first and eCW grants a write contract quickly, flip the order. Defer Epic, which means health-system sales and per-system HL7 work, and Experity, which has no API and is building its own AI coding. Leave FQHCs, home health, and the procedure-heavy specialties to later releases.

### Six checks cover most of the money and most of the risk

HCCs are the diagnosis groups that set Medicare Advantage payments per patient, so for these groups the money is in documenting chronic conditions accurately. MedPAC projects MA payments of about **$16,242 per enrollee in 2026**, so a 0.05 shift in a panel's average risk score is worth roughly $800 per patient per year to the plan. That is 10–25 times the per-patient visit-level uplift **[Inference]** ([Becker's on MedPAC](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/medicare-advantage-spending-14-higher-than-fee-for-service-report/)). It is also the most heavily policed area. CMS will exclude diagnoses from unlinked chart reviews starting with 2027 payments, and Kaiser paid **$556M** in January 2026 over alleged unsupported MA diagnoses ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicare/decoding-medicare-advantage-coding-intensity/); [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/kaiser-permanente-affiliates-pay-556m-resolve-false-claims-act-allegations)). That justifies deferring HCC work and framing it as accuracy support when it comes.

### Every finding becomes a card in the biller's queue

The MVP's primary output is the finding card shown in the worked example: proposed change, direction, dollars, quoted evidence, rule cited, confidence, payer note and actions. It lands in the queue the practice's billers already work. On athenaOne it sits alongside held claims. On eClinicalWorks it starts as a worklist until the write contract lands **[Recommendation]**. A clinician query, the second output, is a short, non-leading question about a genuine documentation gap. It never suggests a diagnosis the patient did not have, which is the conduct OIG's February 2026 Medicare Advantage guidance warns against ([Parker Poe](https://www.parkerpoe.com/news/2026/06/oig-guidance-warns-physician-practices-of-compliance-risks)). The third output is a weekly dashboard. It shows net dollars corrected **in both directions**, and each clinician's E/M distribution against specialty benchmarks, which warns about outlier drift before payer analytics catch it. It also shows acceptance rate and time-to-decision per check, plus a per-clinician education scorecard. Under the hood sits an append-only audit trail recording who or what proposed each change, the model, prompt and rule versions, the evidence, the approver and the timestamp. Turnaround should be measured from signature to finding, with a target median of a few business hours and the 95th percentile under 24 hours, so the product fits inside the billing window **[Recommendation]**.

### A 400-chart adjudicated gold set is the first real asset

No public dataset fits. MIMIC and MDACE are inpatient-centric; MDACE has only **52 professional-fee charts** and requires a PhysioNet license. The public Amazon outpatient set (207 notes) covers ICD-10 only, and ProFees's test set is private ([MDACE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.03859); [Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07629)). The product must build its own **[Recommendation]**. Draw **300–500 de-identified charts** from design partners' shadow-audit pulls. Have two CPC coders label each chart independently, recording final codes, each MDM element's level, the note spans supporting it, and the modifier decision. A senior auditor holding the Certified Professional Medical Auditor (CPMA) or Certified Risk Adjustment Coder (CRC) credential then resolves disagreements. Keep an "agreement" subset and an "adjudicated disagreement" subset and report them separately, as ProFees did ([ProFees](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25007v1)). The effort is roughly 60–150 coder-hours for 400 charts, about **$2,000–$6,000** at in-house loaded wages or up to ~$30,000 at consultant audit rates **[Inference]**.

Synthetic notes are useful for unit tests and adversarial cases, such as a note supporting 99213 billed as 99215, but not for headline metrics. Outpatient-versus-inpatient transfer failures show how much distribution shift matters ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07629)).

| Metric | Definition | MVP bar **[Recommendation]** |
|---|---|---|
| Flag precision | Share of flags the adjudicated label (and later, the customer's coder) agrees with | ≥85% for level flags before a check goes live, reported separately for up and down |
| Recall | Share of adjudicated errors flagged | Reported; precision takes priority at launch |
| Per-check precision and recall | Level, diagnosis, add-on, modifier 25, completeness, NCCI/MUE | Every check gated individually |
| Hierarchical ICD match | Exact plus prefix-level match | Reported to separate near-misses from misses |
| Net dollar impact per 100 charts | Up minus down corrections at PFS or contract rates | Reported monthly |
| Directional balance | Share of corrections and dollars going down vs. up | Reported to customers and compliance |
| Run-to-run stability | Same eval repeated about 5 times | ≤2-point variance |
| Turnaround | Signature to finding | Median a few business hours; 95th percentile <24 hours |
| Time per decision | Coder seconds per flag | Measured; sub-10-second approvals flagged as possible rubber-stamping |

After launch, every accept, edit and reject becomes a new label. Contracts should say plainly whether customer data may improve shared models. Customers' BAAs often prohibit it, and the CPT AI terms constrain it further ([AMA AI FAQ](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/licensing-cpt-ai-faqs)).

## Compliance, pricing and people decide survival more than accuracy

### HIPAA, SOC 2 and False Claims Act exposure set hard requirements

A coding-review vendor is a HIPAA **business associate** from its first pilot with real charts. The regulation's definition names claims processing, data analysis, quality assurance and billing as business-associate functions, and there is no pilot exemption ([45 CFR 160.103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)). It needs a **business associate agreement (BAA)** with every customer, and flow-down BAAs with every subcontractor that touches **protected health information (PHI)**, meaning any patient-identifiable health or billing data. That includes cloud, LLM and logging vendors ([45 CFR 164.502](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)). It must limit data pulls to the minimum necessary and notify customers of a breach within **60 days** of discovery ([45 CFR 164.410](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.410)).

The proposed HIPAA Security Rule overhaul (published January 2025) is not final, and its target date has moved to July 2027. Buyers already ask for its controls: multi-factor authentication, encryption and asset inventories ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/07/hhs-updates-hipaa-rulemaking-timeframes)). Small and mid-size groups usually accept a BAA, a security questionnaire, a pen test and a SOC 2 Type I report. **SOC 2** is an independent auditor's report on security controls: Type I covers design at a point in time, Type II covers operation over months. Health systems expect Type II, often HITRUST (a healthcare-specific security certification), and $5M or more of combined technology errors-and-omissions (E&O) and cyber insurance. A first-year SOC 2 Type II costs about **$25,000–$50,000** all in, a pen test **$5,000–$30,000**, and HITRUST i1 **$70,000–$200,000**. Houston Methodist's vendor insurance requirements specify $5M of combined Tech E&O and cyber cover **[Vendor estimates]** ([Drata](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/cost); [SOC2Auditors.org](https://soc2auditors.org/soc-2-auditors-startups/); [Astra](https://www.getastra.com/blog/penetration-testing/cost/); [BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/hitrust-certification); [Houston Methodist](https://it.houstonmethodist.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/IT-Insurance-Requirements.pdf)).

The bigger exposure is the **False Claims Act (FCA)**, the federal law that makes it illegal to knowingly submit, or cause someone to submit, a false claim to a government program. It carries treble damages and per-claim penalties, and "knowingly" includes reckless disregard. Whistleblowers ("relators") can sue on the government's behalf and share in recoveries. The provider stays responsible for every claim, and "'the AI suggested it' is not a fantastic defense" ([Tucker Ellis](https://www.tuckerellis.com/alerts/avoiding-false-claims-act-landmines-in-ai-assisted-coding-and-medical-billing/); [Health Law Attorney Blog](https://www.healthlawattorneyblog.com/ai-is-billing-your-patients-who-holds-the-bag-the-rise-of-ambient-billing-and-coding-tools-and-the-legal-minefield-your-practice-is-walking-into/)). Vendors are not immune. EHR vendors have paid nine-figure FCA settlements, such as Practice Fusion's ~$145M, for *causing* false claims. Three precedents sit closest to AI coding **[Authoritative]**. **UCHealth** paid **$23M** in 2024 after an *automated rule* coded emergency visits at the top level whenever vital signs were checked more times than the patient's hours in the department; an in-house coder blew the whistle after CMS flagged the system as a "high outlier". **Independent Health and its subsidiary DxID** settled for up to **$98M**, and DxID's former CEO personally paid **$2M**; DxID mined records for extra diagnoses on contingency, reportedly keeping up to 20% of what it generated. And **"one-way" chart reviews**, which added codes but did not delete unsupported ones, underlie the pending DOJ case against Anthem and the Cigna ($172M) and DaVita ($270M) settlements.

Sources: [Phillips & Cohen](https://www.phillipsandcohen.com/practice-fusion-145m-settlement-more-ehr-kickbacks-fraud-cases-future/); [DOJ UCHealth](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/uchealth-agrees-pay-23m-resolve-allegations-fraudulent-billing-emergency-department-visits); [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/medicare-advantage-fraud-lawsuit-settlement-new-york/); [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2406/2026-01-13-medicare-advantage-under-microscope-enforcement). No case was found against an independent AI coding vendor, so vendor liability remains unsettled. Enforcement capacity is growing: a new DOJ fraud division, data-mining whistleblowers filing 45%+ of qui tams since fiscal 2024, and record recoveries ([Husch Blackwell](https://www.huschblackwell.com/newsandinsights/false-claims-act-insights2026-mid-year-fca-update-new-doj-infrastructure-trials-and-the-rise-of-data-mining-whistleblowers)).

The MVP's compliance design follows directly **[Recommendation]**. Every level, diagnosis and add-on change is **suggest-only**; auto-apply is limited to deterministic fixes such as POS/modifier consistency and NCCI-indicated modifiers, and only on customer opt-in with monitoring. The product **never amends the medical record**; documentation gaps go to non-leading clinician queries. It reports **findings in both directions** with equal prominence, and it watches for rubber-stamping, such as coders approving flags in under a few seconds. It keeps an **immutable audit trail with versioned models and rules**, which matters because DOJ's 2024 compliance guidance asks prosecutors to assess how companies manage AI risk ([Jones Day](https://jonesday.com/de/insights/2024/10/doj-updates-corporate-compliance-program-guidance-with-a-focus-on-ai)). Accuracy claims must be substantiated: Texas's attorney general settled with health-AI firm Pieces Technologies over misleading accuracy metrics ([Tucker Ellis](https://www.tuckerellis.com/alerts/avoiding-false-claims-act-landmines-in-ai-assisted-coding-and-medical-billing/)). **No pricing is tied to uplift.** OIG's 1998 guidance for billing companies warned that "percentage billing arrangements may increase the risk of upcoding," and it asks for pre- and post-submission claim review and periodic review of "software edits" ([OIG 1998 CPG](https://oig.hhs.gov/documents/compliance-guidance/805/thirdparty.pdf)). Contracts should cap liability at about 12 months of fees, with a higher cap for data breaches. They should state that the customer keeps final coding responsibility, commit the vendor to audit support (evidence packets for payer audits), and exclude FCA penalties from indemnity.

Three regulatory regimes mostly do not reach the product. Billing and claims software is excluded from FDA's medical-device definition by statute ([21 U.S.C. 360j(o)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/360j)). HTI-1's algorithm-transparency rules bind certified EHR developers, not third-party tools ([ONC DSI guide](https://healthit.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/DSI-Criterion-Resource-Guide_508.pdf)). State AI laws mostly target patient-facing or clinical uses. Colorado's amended AI Act (effective January 1, 2027) needs a counsel opinion before then ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-ai-act-amended-and-effective-date-delayed)).

### Price per chart, guarantee on net accuracy, never a cut of uplift

Nobody in this market publishes prices. Charta's is the anchor, and it is an **[Estimate]** triangulated in earlier coordinator analysis from four public signals, since no public source discloses it. First, Charta claims a "98% lower cost per chart" than manual review and compliance audits "at 1% of traditional audit cost" ([Charta](https://www.chartahealth.com/ai-chart-review); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/charta-health-8-1-million-seed-secured-for-automating-chart-review-with-ai/)); against $20–$40 manual audit charts, that implies a floor of roughly **$0.20–$0.80 per chart**. Second, an 11% claimed uplift divided by an investor-claimed **11:1 ROI** implies a fee of about 1% of revenue, or roughly **$1–$1.50 per primary care visit** ([Bain Capital Ventures](https://baincapitalventures.com/insight/charting-a-new-era-how-charta-health-is-transforming-medical-chart-review/)). Third, an urgent care customer that replaced a ~$90,000-a-year offshore coding team reported a 1.25x cost-savings ROI, implying a fee near $72,000 a year, although the same page also says "$90,000 per month" ([Charta urgent care case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/audit-proof-compliance-urgent-care)). Fourth, about **$500,000 in revenue within 60 days** came from "several six-figure contracts" ([Business Insider via AOL](https://www.aol.com/engineers-raised-8-1-million-140002032.html)).

The estimate is **about $1 per chart reviewed (range $0.50–$2)**, or 1–2% of visit revenue. That implies annual contracts of roughly $50,000–$150,000 for small groups, $200,000–$500,000 for mid-size multi-site groups and $500,000–$1.5M for large networks. Charta's early model reportedly charged only "for approved findings" ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/charta-health-8-1-million-seed-secured-for-automating-chart-review-with-ai/)). If that model is still a contingency, a fee near 10% of found revenue would square with the 11:1 claim.

The defensible position is the one EHR-native and scribe coding cannot take credibly: an **independent second set of eyes** **[Inference]**. The Charta overlap argues for a narrower vertical (primary care plus one or two EHRs) and a compliance-first message, rather than a horizontal clone.

| Tier | What falls in it |
|---|---|
| **Authoritative** | CERT error rates and root causes; 2026 PFS prices and conversion factor; AMA MDM and time rules; JAMA G2211 uptake; OIG audit findings; ONC (g)(10) read-only scope; HIPAA business-associate, minimum-necessary and breach rules; FDA statutory exclusion; DOJ settlements; BLS wages; CMS 2027 proposals (proposed, not final) |

## Conclusion

The economics of this product are decided by people and permissions, not by models. LLM inference costs cents per chart. Human review of each flag, EHR partner access and per-practice activation set both unit margin and time to revenue. That changes what an MVP should optimize. The target is flag precision and turnaround inside a three-day billing window, not raw recall or model sophistication. The scarcest assets are an adjudicated gold set and a sponsoring design partner, not a prompt. The honest value proposition is also smaller and sturdier than the market's headline. A reviewer that corrects in both directions, applied to primary care at Medicare rates, plausibly nets about 3–4% of visit revenue rather than 11%. At roughly $1 per chart that is still a four-to-one return. It is also the only version that survives payer analytics, FCA scrutiny and a compliance officer's review.

The competitive picture points the same way. Once athenahealth, Epic and Experity sell coding inside the EHR, the open job is checking the coder, whether a clinician, an outsourced team or the EHR's own AI, before the claim ships. An independent, evidence-citing, two-directional reviewer with versioned, date-keyed rules fits that job. It is also the role regulators already demand when they ask for pre-submission review, periodic validation of software edits and no incentive to upcode. The 2027 fee-schedule proposals sharpen the point. Moving G2211 to a modifier, paying the lower-valued service at 50% when a visit and a procedure share a day, and cutting the conversion factor would make rule agility, more than model choice, the product's lasting advantage.
