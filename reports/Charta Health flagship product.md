# Pre-Bill AI Chart Review Powers Charta Health

Charta Health sells one LLM-based chart-review engine through two storefronts. Providers see seven "solutions": Autonomous coding, Revenue discovery, Payer compliance, Clinical quality, Risk adjustment, Provider feedback and Clinical documentation improvement. Payers see six "products" built on Charta's Smart Scan evidence engine: retrospective and prospective risk adjustment, automated second-pass QA, clinical chart intelligence, RADV audit management and HEDIS analytics. The line that most likely brings in the majority of revenue is the original provider product, **pre-billing AI chart review**, now marketed mainly as Autonomous coding, Revenue discovery and Payer compliance. It pulls every closed encounter note from an outpatient group's EHR and runs a customer-specific set of LLM "analyses" against coding, payer and documentation rules. Before the claim goes out, it then does one of three things: autocorrects the codes in the EHR, messages the provider, or queues the chart for a human. Charta is private and has never disclosed revenue, ARR or a revenue split, so the majority-revenue call is our inference, held at **moderate confidence (our judgment: roughly 70%)**. The provider line appears in both funding announcements, the athenahealth distribution deal, 9 of 10 case studies and every named testimonial. The payer suite surfaced only in 2026 and has no named customer. The main risk to the call is concentration: Charta's own recruiting copy describes "a handful of customers," so a few large health-plan contracts could shift the mix quickly. Almost everything known about how the product works and what it delivers comes from Charta, its investors, or customer quotes hosted on Charta's site. No KLAS report, peer review or analyst has validated any outcome, including the headline **11% average revenue uplift**.

The two halves run the same engine in opposite directions. The provider line works **prospectively and pre-bill**. It reads live EHR notes as they close, writes corrections back before the claim exists, and lets the customer decide how much human oversight to keep. The payer line works **retrospectively**. It ingests charts a health plan has already retrieved, parses them by date of service, and always routes AI-cited codes to human coders for validation ([Smart Scan explainer](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/smart-scan-for-retro-coding-vendor-evaluation); [Payer case study](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/maximizing-hcc-capture-audit-readiness)). The shared core is LLM extraction of clinical evidence with citations. "Smart Scan" is the brand that connects the provider-side Risk adjustment solution to the payer suite.

The commercial model is only partly visible. At seed, Charta said providers "pay only for approved findings" with "guaranteed ROI" ([Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/charta-health-8-1-million-seed-secured-for-automating-chart-review-with-ai)). On balance the evidence favors the provider line. Pre-bill review was the product that produced Charta's first revenue, its first six-figure contracts, both funding rounds, and every named reference customer. The payer suite was not mentioned in any 2025 material. Its public footprint as of October 2026 is a set of product pages, one anonymized case study, a sales-lead hire and upcoming webinars, which is the profile of a line still building pipeline. A product line launched in 2026 would need payer contracts larger than the entire installed provider base, signed within months, to account for most revenue. That outcome is possible but not the base case.

The strongest counter-argument concerns size, not timing. Retrospective risk adjustment and RADV contracts with Medicare Advantage plans are large, and Charta's own language ("a handful of customers") suggests a small enough base that two or three plan contracts could outweigh dozens of outpatient groups. The About page leading with payer metrics, and an AI-engineer role building HCC, CDPS and CRG coding six or more months before the payer pages appeared, both show that management is investing heavily on the payer side. Recruiting copy is rhetorical, and "a handful" sits awkwardly beside Charta's Series A claim of use "in 43 states" ([EIN Presswire](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/832598993/charta-health-raises-22-million-series-a-to-rebuild-the-backbone-of-healthcare-operations-with-ai)) and ten published case studies, so the concentration signal itself is uncertain. We therefore put the probability that provider pre-bill review is the majority-revenue line **as of October 2026 at roughly 70%**. That number is our judgment, not a disclosed figure, and it falls as the payer suite matures. Three developments would weaken the call: a named payer customer, payer case studies with realized rather than projected ROI, or a funding announcement that leads with risk adjustment.

Within the provider line the split cannot be resolved further. Several case studies are titled around compliance yet report revenue metrics, such as "audit-proof compliance… leading to revenue discovery" ([Case studies](https://www.chartahealth.com/case-studies)). Customers also describe adopting Charta as their "primary coding tool to autonomously review and code 100% of patient charts" ([FL FQHC case study](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/capturing-revenue-and-ensuring-compliance-for-a-20-clinic-fqhc-in-south-florida)). Together these indicate that Autonomous coding, Revenue discovery and Payer compliance are bought as one platform deployment, not as three revenue lines. The rest of this report treats them as a single flagship: **pre-billing AI chart review**.

## A closed note becomes a corrected claim within 24 hours

The flagship sits in what CEO Justin Liu calls the space "between clinical documentation and claim submission" ([Stedi Spotlight](https://www.stedi.com/blog/spotlight-justin-liu-charta)). That window is the stretch after a clinician signs a note and before the biller releases the claim. Charta's homepage compresses the mechanism into one sentence: "Every time a provider closes a note in your EHR, Charta identifies the encounter type and runs the analysis modules designed for that type of note" ([Charta homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/)). The subsections below follow a chart through that pipeline. Unless a passage is marked as inference or observation, the mechanics come from Charta's own pages and are vendor-stated.

### Eight weeks of forward-deployed setup precede the first live chart

Each deployment is built partly by hand. The sales motion often begins with Charta analyzing a sample of the prospect's historical charts to size the leakage. At one primary care group, for example, it found "at least 12%" of charts had underleveled E/M codes ([Primary care case study](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/automated-revenue-discovery-primary-care)). The seed-era pitch deck claimed a "100% close rate from proof-of-concept to signed contract" ([Business Insider via AOL](https://www.aol.com/engineers-raised-8-1-million-140002032.html)). Charta's buyer's guide then lays out the sequence ([Buyer's guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-chart-review-buyers-guide)). Discovery ends in a signed BAA and mutual NDA. In scoping, a Charta implementation specialist reviews how the customer has configured its EHR while the customer's billing specialist walks through current coding and billing workflows. Contracting produces a scope of work. Charta then builds a custom EHR integration "that adapts to the ways you've configured and use your specific instance of your EHR." Next comes two to eight weeks of configuring "analyses" and "audit modules," drawn from "our library of analyses built for similar practices" or designed new with an assigned AI engineer. A test batch of sample charts follows, with stakeholder feedback and "additional training," and the process ends with go-live "across your entire patient encounter population."

Charta says primary care, urgent care and behavioral health groups can go live "in as little as eight weeks." A separate 60-day plan places integration, baseline benchmarking, provider-feedback configuration and the first review cycle in weeks 4–8 ([Charta RCM go-live article](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/how-to-get-an-rcm-tool-approved-and-live-before-fall)). The fastest documented build was a working prototype for a large primary care customer in about six days ([BCV](https://baincapitalventures.com/insight/charting-a-new-era-how-charta-health-is-transforming-medical-chart-review/)). Each customer keeps "a dedicated, CPC-certified AI engineer" after launch to update the setup "as payer mix, payer rules, CMS guidelines, and state requirements change" ([Charta RCM tools guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-medical-coding-tools-rcm-guide)).

### Encounter classification feeds a dozen or more LLM analyses per note

After the pull, Charta classifies the encounter type and runs the matching audit module. The homepage example shows a "primary care note identified, 10 coding analyses and 8 clinical quality analyses running" ([Homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/)). A national behavioral health customer runs "12 bespoke analyses every time a provider closes a note" ([Behavioral health case study](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/documentation-compliance-nationwide-scale)). Charta describes the method as LLMs that "review any type of clinical documentation, extract meaning, and run sophisticated, custom-designed analysis modules." It contrasts this with "rules-based engines that rely on natural language processing and user-generated rules," and says each deployment is built "to replicate the types of reviews you'd ask a human reviewer to conduct" ([AI chart review FAQ](https://www.chartahealth.com/ai-chart-review)). Each note is evaluated "simultaneously against coding standards, payer-specific coder and documentation requirements, clinical documentation integrity guidelines, and clinical quality measures" ([Charta blog](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-chart-review-back-office-tech-stack)). An individual analysis can be as simple as checking for a timely provider signature or as complex as judging medical necessity given age and family history ([Buyer's guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-chart-review-buyers-guide)). The checks Charta has documented in practice include:

The model layer is the least disclosed part of the product. Job postings mention "both proprietary and open-source large language models" ([Ashby: Senior SWE](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/chartahealth/8b09b002-9ff1-4209-8291-7673a1e51146)). No model vendor is named anywhere. Charta never mentions NCCI, MUE or LCD/NCD content. Our inference is that payer-specific modifier, bundling and policy logic is encoded as configuration around the LLM calls, not licensed from an edits database. The architecture is best described as an encounter-type classifier feeding a library of LLM-prompted checks, each roughly one line of an auditor's checklist. A policy layer then routes the results.

### Every finding exits through one of three routes

Routing is chosen by the customer: "direct autocorrect in your EHR, send automated notes to providers, or queue charts for human intervention" ([Homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/)). After that come reporting and archiving.

| Output | Who receives it | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| EHR autocorrect / write-back | Billing workflow | Corrected or validated codes pushed back into the EHR before claim release. On eClinicalWorks, Charta "pushes corrected or validated codes back into the billing workflow"; on athenahealth, it "pushes accurately coded charts back into the billing" workflow ([eCW guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-medical-coding-tool-eclinicalworks); [athenahealth guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-coding-tools-athenahealth-2026)) |
| Provider message | Treating clinician | "Messages with actionable steps for remediating documentation directly in the EMR," sent while the chart is still open ([Behavioral health case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/documentation-compliance-nationwide-scale)) |
| Human work queue | Coders, billers, QA staff | Problem charts for review. At one FQHC, results were "returned to the billing team within 24 hours" and claims corrected "the same day, before they reached billing" ([FL FQHC](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/capturing-revenue-and-ensuring-compliance-for-a-20-clinic-fqhc-in-south-florida)) |
| Audit archive | Compliance | "An archive of clear, auditable rationales for each billing code and the corresponding documentation" ([Revenue cycle page](https://chartahealth.com/teams/revenue-cycle)) |
| Dashboards | RCM, clinical and executive leaders | RVU-per-encounter uplift (11.5% in the screenshot), documentation compliance scores, provider performance rankings, with drill-down to "visual citations" ([Homepage](https://www.chartahealth.com/); [Outpatient team guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-medical-chart-review-for-outpatient-practices-team-guide)) |
| Scorecards | Individual clinicians | "Automated scorecards" comparing care delivery, documentation and coding across sites and providers ([Provider feedback](https://chartahealth.com/solutions/provider-feedback)) |

The loop closes inside the EHR or practice-management system, not at the clearinghouse, and no source mentions claim-scrubber integration. Where integration is deepest, Charta says users never open its software. Of ModMed it says: "Your team doesn't have to log into another platform" ([Dermatology page](https://chartahealth.com/for/dermatology)). A separate web application does exist. We observed that app.chartahealth.com is a Next.js application with Clerk authentication, Sentry and PostHog, served from AWS App Runner in us-east-2 alongside an authenticated API ([DNS lookup, app](https://dns.google/resolve?name=app.chartahealth.com&type=CNAME); [DNS lookup, api](https://dns.google/resolve?name=api.chartahealth.com&type=CNAME)). Downstream, the output changes who does the work. At one urgent care group the coding team went "from a team of 4 medical coders to 1." At a primary care group, E/M leveling and CPT coding moved to Charta and the offshore team shifted to documentation quality. In home health, QA staff "now focus exclusively on high-risk variances flagged by AI" ([Urgent care E/M case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/em-lift-urgent-care); [Primary care case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/automated-revenue-discovery-primary-care); [Home health case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/optimizing-clinical-compliance)).

### Pods of engineers keep each deployment running

Job postings, which are operational documents and not marketing, show what is behind the pipeline. "Every account is mapped to an owning engineering pod" of up to five engineers. Customer engineers handle failures such as "a chart that wasn't processed, a code that didn't appear, an integration that stopped sending data, an output that doesn't match what the clinician documented." They also "re-run failed jobs" and answer "did Charta do this?" with evidence ([Ashby: Customer Engineer](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/chartahealth/92849d59-cbcc-4d37-b9a8-18f1fb374f58); [Ashby: Tech Lead Manager](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/chartahealth/50864962-2ba6-40fd-bfa4-afb9d4b98229)). Technical Success Managers own deployment health, QBRs and renewals ([Ashby: TSM](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/chartahealth/9754fa43-7399-4305-bf6d-933ca71b9818)). Management says plainly that this does not yet scale: the goal is to "turn bespoke customer builds into scalable, repeatable product" ([Ashby: Staff PM](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/chartahealth/0e2b8e22-e7d0-4ed8-9d19-0e60f599de27)). On compliance, Charta states it is "HIPAA, SOC 2 and GDPR compliant" and offers reports on request ([AI chart review FAQ](https://www.chartahealth.com/ai-chart-review)). It does not say whether its SOC 2 is Type I or Type II, publishes no trust center, and was hiring a "Founding Security Reliability Engineer" in April 2026 ([Ashby](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/charta-health/6b71a01f-a8c7-4fb3-a9e5-7394469cf14e)).

## Charta sells 11% more revenue, but no third party has checked

Charta's value proposition has three parts: recapture revenue lost to undercoding, replace or stretch coding and audit labor, and stop compliance problems before they become denials or clawbacks. The economics depend on coverage. Investors describe manual audits as sampling about 1–2% of charts, often weeks after billing ([Madrona](https://www.madrona.com/charting-healthcares-ai-transformation-investing-in-charta-health)). Charta's customers report prior coverage of 10% (Family Care Center) and 0.1% (a national behavioral health organization) ([FCC case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/compliance-assurance-behavioral-health); [Behavioral health case](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/documentation-compliance-nationwide-scale)). Charta's pitch is that AI review at a fraction of manual cost makes 100% coverage affordable, and that whatever 100% coverage finds is pure upside. Every figure in the ledger below is **vendor- or investor-stated**. We found no independent verification of any of them.

The gaps matter as much as the claims. Charta publishes **no accuracy, precision or agreement-with-coder figure for autonomous coding or E/M leveling**. Its only accuracy number, 98%, applies to payer HCC coding that human coders validate ([Retrospective risk adjustment](https://chartahealth.com/products/retrospective-risk-adjustment)). It publishes no denial-rate change, days in A/R, clean-claim rate, dollars of clawback avoided, or acceptance and override rates for its suggestions. Its ROI multiples (1.25x, 5x, 11:1, and a projected 26x for a payer) cannot be compared with one another, because pricing is undisclosed and each multiple is computed differently.

Most of the documented revenue comes from **higher coding intensity per visit**: E/M levels rising, more add-on codes, more modifiers. That is the same economic mechanism payers attacked in September 2026. Blue Cross Blue Shield Association research estimated that AI-assisted billing tools added about **$942M over two years** to Blue plan costs, with BCBSA's Luke Chalker arguing that "AI is identifying more billable conditions, not sicker patients." CodaMetrix's CEO countered that such tools capture what providers "couldn't without technical assistance" ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/insurers-say-ai-could-add-billions-in-health-costs-billing-companies-disag/831497/)). Charta is not named in that research, which focuses on hospital coding. Its framing still anticipates the attack. Product pages speak of "revenue integrity," the "highest defensible level" and "guaranteed payer compliance" rather than uplift alone, and the clearest compliance proof points run in both directions: flagging overcoding in urgent care, and surfacing unsupported HCC "deletes" for payers ([Revenue discovery](https://www.chartahealth.com/solutions/revenue-discovery); [Payer case study](https://chartahealth.com/case-studies/maximizing-hcc-capture-audit-readiness)). There is one tension. Investors describe a system that flags issues and explains its reasoning "with humans retaining final approval" ([Madrona](https://www.madrona.com/charting-healthcares-ai-transformation-investing-in-charta-health)), while the product now sells "fully autonomous medical coding" and EHR autocorrect. That shifts accountability for submitted codes onto configuration choices the customer makes with Charta's engineer.

Competitive context sharpens the point about validation. SmarterDx, the closest analog in pre-bill review, works on inpatient DRGs and holds a **KLAS ROI-validation report** from February 2026 ([KLAS](https://klasresearch.com/report/smarterdx-smarterprebill-roi-validations-2026-improving-documentation-completeness-quality-and-revenue/3860)). It is now part of Smarter Technologies, which expected more than $800M in revenue at formation ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250518030750/en/New-Mountain-Capital-Forms-Smarter-Technologies-through-Combination-of-SmarterDx-Thoughtful.ai-and-Access-Healthcare)). CodaMetrix says it automates coding for more than 500 hospitals and health systems ([Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/insurers-say-ai-could-add-billions-in-health-costs-billing-companies-disag/831497/)). Charta's niche is the segment those vendors underserve: multi-site outpatient groups on athenahealth, eCW or Experity, in what seed-round coverage called "high-volume, low-reimbursement specialties." Liu has said Charta competes there on breadth of use cases and per-client customization, explicitly positioning against inpatient-focused SmarterDx ([Business Insider via AOL](https://www.aol.com/engineers-raised-8-1-million-140002032.html)). Its athenahealth distribution partner also sells native coding tools that Charta's own guide lists as a competitor ([Charta RCM tools guide](https://www.chartahealth.com/resources/ai-medical-coding-tools-rcm-guide)).

The practical consequence is that a buyer, investor or competitor can be confident about *what Charta says the product does* and fairly confident about *what Charta chooses to sell*. They cannot be confident about *how well it works*. Even the architecture is reconstructed from image alt text, job postings and intern blogs rather than documentation. Charta publishes no API docs, product changelog or demo video, and the first public payer platform demo is not scheduled until November 19, 2026 ([Charta payer page](https://www.chartahealth.com/payer)).

## Conclusion

Charta's flagship is less a coding engine than a way of turning an audit checklist into software. Its durable asset is the library of customer-specific LLM "analyses," together with the forward-deployed engineers who translate each practice's audit checklist and payer rules into running checks within weeks. That explains both the strong early traction (a prototype in six days, POCs on the customer's own charts) and the open question behind the 2026 hiring push: whether bespoke builds can become a repeatable platform before services costs cap margins. The move from "pre-bill audit" to "autonomous coding" matters more than the rebrand suggests. Once Charta originates the codes, it is no longer advising on the claim; it is effectively producing it, which raises the stakes on the accuracy figures it has never published.

The revenue mix is at an inflection that outside observers can monitor even without disclosed numbers. Today's evidence favors the provider line, but the payer suite is where Charta is now hiring senior sales, publishing metrics and scheduling demos, and per-contract economics there are larger. Payer pressure on AI-driven coding intensity also makes the provider line's headline 11% uplift harder to sell than the payer line's "audit-proof deletes." The first named health-plan customer, a payer case study with realized ROI, or a funding announcement led by risk adjustment would each signal that the majority-revenue line has changed, or is about to.
