# Anatomy of the US Outpatient Professional-Fee Coding and Documentation Error Problem, and the Best Wedge Segment for a Pre-Bill AI Chart Review Entrant

Research date: 2026-10-21. Scope: US only. Older data is flagged with its year.

Source-quality legend used throughout:
- **[AUTH]** = authoritative: CMS, HHS-OIG, BLS, HRSA, MedPAC, AMA CPT guideline text, or peer-reviewed journal.
- **[SURVEY]** = industry association or trade survey (MGMA, Experian, Premier, AAPC, UCA): useful, but self-selected samples.

## Q1. The workflow: visit → note → code assignment → charge entry → scrubber/clearinghouse → payer → payment or denial. Where does pre-bill review sit, and how much time is there before submission?

### Takeaway
Pre-bill review sits between "the clinician signs the note and a code is proposed" and "the claim leaves the practice to the clearinghouse". The typical window is short: MGMA reported a median of about **3 days** from date of service to claim submission (2019 data), and many practices submit most claims within 3 days. A pre-bill product therefore has to return findings in hours to roughly 1–2 business days, or it lengthens the time to get paid. Most professional coding is still selected by the clinician at the point of care (strong but dated evidence: 2011), so the "coder" in the loop for many practices is the doctor, NP or PA. That is where both under-coding and over-coding start.

### Cited Findings

#### Plain-language glossary (standard definitions, used in the rest of these notes)
- **Professional fee (pro-fee) claim**: the bill a clinician (physician, nurse practitioner (NP), physician assistant (PA)) sends for their own work, on the CMS-1500/837P claim format. Hospitals, FQHCs and home health agencies bill facility ("institutional") claims on a different format (UB-04/837I) and are counted in different CMS buckets — [CMS CERT 2025 supplemental report, p.1–2](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf) [AUTH].
- **CPT / HCPCS codes**: 5-character codes that say *what service was done* (e.g., 99214 = established-patient office visit, moderate complexity). CPT is maintained by the AMA. HCPCS Level II "G-codes" (e.g., G2211) are Medicare-created codes. **ICD-10-CM codes** say *why* (the diagnosis).
- **E/M (evaluation and management)**: the family of codes for visits where a clinician evaluates and manages a patient (office visits are 99202–99205 for new patients and 99211–99215 for established patients) — [AMA CPT E/M guidelines (2023 edition)](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf) [AUTH].
- **Modifier**: a 2-character suffix that changes the meaning of a code (e.g., -25 = "significant, separately identifiable E/M on the same day as a procedure", -95/-93 = telehealth video/audio). See Q3.
- **RVU / conversion factor**: Medicare prices each code in relative value units (RVUs) and multiplies them by a dollar "conversion factor" ($33.4009 for most clinicians in 2026) — [CMS PFS relative value file RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/payment/fee-schedules/physician/pfs-relative-value-files/rvu26d) [AUTH].
- **Claim scrubber / clearinghouse**: software (in the billing system or at an intermediary "clearinghouse") that checks a claim's format and payer edits (e.g., invalid code pairs, missing modifiers) before sending it to the payer. Scrubbers check the *claim*. They do not read the *clinical note*, which is the gap pre-bill chart review fills. (Definition; standard industry usage.)
- **Denial**: the payer refuses payment, in full or in part. **Downcoding** by a payer means it pays a lower level than billed.
- **Charge lag**: days from date of service to charge entry. **Submission lag**: days from date of service to claim transmission. Definitions vary by vendor — [PCC benchmark post](https://chipsblog.pcc.com/claim-submission-delay-benchmark) [VENDOR]. MGMA defines charge-posting lag as days between the date the patient was seen and when the claim was posted for third-party payment — [MGMA 2019 letter to Congress](https://www.mgma.com/getkaiasset/6a638b39-cce7-4b9a-83a9-04aca5a1c9f3/12.09.2019_Letter-Opposing-Section-305-FINAL.pdf) [SURVEY].
- **CERT**: CMS's Comprehensive Error Rate Testing program. It randomly samples paid Medicare fee-for-service claims, requests the medical records, and has reviewers judge whether the payment was correct. It is the main federal measure of coding and documentation error — [CMS CERT 2025 report](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf) [AUTH].

#### Who assigns the code
- In a 2012 HHS-OIG study of 2011 data (2,000 sampled Medicare physicians), most physicians reported assigning E/M codes manually themselves, with a minority having staff assign codes. None of the physicians with EHRs used the EHR's automatic coding module — [HHS-OIG, Use of EHR Systems in 2011 Among Medicare Physicians Providing E/M Services](https://oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2012/use-of-electronic-health-record-systems-in-2011-among-medicare-physicians-providing-evaluation-and-management-services) [AUTH, **dated 2012**]. Secondary summaries put the split at 88% self/12% staff — [Becker's ASC](https://www.beckersasc.com/asc-coding-billing-and-collections/medicare-physicians-still-code-manually-despite-availability-of-ehr/) (verify the exact split in the OIG report).
- Since 2021, the CPT office-visit rules let the clinician choose the level by medical decision making (MDM) or by total time on the date of the encounter, with history and exam no longer used to set the level — [AMA E/M office visit changes 2021](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2020-04/e-m-office-visit-changes.pdf) [AUTH]. That made clinician self-selection the default design.
- Charta Health (the reference product) says that traditionally only about 1% of charts get reviewed, and only after billing, while its product reviews 100% of charts before billing. It is on the athenahealth Marketplace (athena network ~160,000 providers) — [Bain Capital Ventures](https://baincapitalventures.com/insight/charting-a-new-era-how-charta-health-is-transforming-medical-chart-review/); [HIT Consultant, Jul 2025](https://hitconsultant.net/2025/07/22/charta-health-secures-22m-ai-powered-automated-chart-review/) [VENDOR].

#### How practices audit today
- MGMA Stat poll (April 2022): 47% of medical group leaders audit charts and coding internally, 34% use both internal and external audits, 13% external only, and 6% do not audit at all — [MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stats/the-most-asked-questions-about-medical-coding-audits-following-the-2021-e-m-and-mdm-changes) [SURVEY, 2022].
- Typical audit samples are small and retrospective. AAPC guidance cites about 10 charts per provider every 6–12 months as a common baseline — [AAPC Part B Insider](https://www.aapc.com/codes/coding-newsletters/my-part-b-coding-alert/audits-dont-start-auditing-your-em-visits-without-a-baseline-article) [SURVEY/trade]. Consultants describe 10–30+ charts per provider per period, or 2 charts per doctor per quarter — [AAPC forum thread](https://www.aapc.com/discuss/threads/help-what-is-of-charts-needed-for-audit.193024/?view=votes) [anecdotal].

#### Charge lag (the time window)
- MGMA data: the median lag between date of service and claim submission is about 3 days — [MGMA 2019 letter](https://www.mgma.com/getkaiasset/6a638b39-cce7-4b9a-83a9-04aca5a1c9f3/12.09.2019_Letter-Opposing-Section-305-FINAL.pdf) [SURVEY, **2019**].
- In a 2020 pediatric benchmark, 63.8% of claims were submitted in 3 days or fewer — [PCC](https://chipsblog.pcc.com/claim-submission-delay-benchmark) [VENDOR, 2020]. Vendor guidance targets charge entry within 24 hours of the visit — same source.

#### What happens downstream (payer edits and denials)
- Experian Health State of Claims 2025 (250 revenue cycle leaders, June–July 2025): 41% of providers have denial rates ≥10% (up from 30% in 2022 and 38% in 2024). 54% say claim errors are increasing. 68% say clean claims are harder to submit than a year ago. Top denial causes: missing or inaccurate data 50%, authorizations 35%, registration errors 32%. 90% of denials are reworked with some human review. 14% use AI in claims — [Experian press release, Sep 22 2025](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2025/experian-health-s-3rd-annual-state-of-claims-survey-finds-denial) [SURVEY].
- MGMA Stat (March 2024): about 60% of medical group leaders said denial rates rose versus the prior year. 29% said flat, 11% lower — [MGMA Stat](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stat/strategic-improvements-in-your-rcm-to-reduce-your-practices-claim-denials) [SURVEY]. The MGMA DataDive single-specialty first-submission denial rate was 8% (2023 data) per a secondary summary — [MGMA data story](https://www.mgma.com/mgma-stats/6-keys-to-addressing-denials-in-your-medical-practice-s-revenue-cycle) [SURVEY, not opened directly].
- Medicare FFS 2025 E/M lines: denied services were about 2.7–5.2% of submitted office E/M services across the specialties I pulled (e.g., family practice 4.6%, internal medicine 4.2%, dermatology 2.7%, psychiatry 5.2%) — [DERIVED from CMS Physician/Supplier Procedure Summary (PSPS) 2025](https://data.cms.gov/data-api/v1/dataset/aa28896d-e502-4742-8b8d-65177ff45c7a/data). Method: sum of PSPS_DENIED_SERVICES_CNT ÷ PSPS_SUBMITTED_SERVICE_CNT for HCPCS 99202–99205 and 99211–99215 by provider specialty code. Cells suppressed with "*" were excluded.
- Commercial payers are adding algorithmic E/M downcoding. Cigna's policy R49 (announced for Oct 1, 2025) said it would review claims with high-level E/M codes and could adjust them down one level when AMA guidelines are not met. State and national medical societies objected that the algorithm was not disclosed — [California Medical Association](https://www.cmadocs.org/newsroom/news/view/ArticleId/50953/CMA-urges-Cigna-to-withdraw-unlawful-and-burdensome-downcoding-policy). Its status is contested: Cigna paused it at least in California, per [AAFP FPM](https://www.aafp.org/fpm/blogs/getting-paid/cigna-downcoding-em) and [StreamlineMD](https://streamlinemd.com/cigna-temporarily-pauses-its-e-m-coding-and-accuracy-policy/).
- Premier survey (516 hospitals, claims from CY2022): about 15% of claims to private payers were initially denied. Providers spent an average of $43.84 per claim to contest denials (≈$19.7B/yr nationally) — [Healthcare Finance News](https://www.healthcarefinancenews.com/news/private-payers-deny-15-claims-survey-finds) [SURVEY, hospitals not physician practices, **2022 data**].
- MDaudit 2025 benchmark: outpatient coding-related denials grew 26% (after +126% the prior year). Professional coding-related denials grew about 7.5% — [MDaudit 2025 benchmark report](https://mdaudit.com/resource/report/2025-benchmark-report/); [MDaudit page](https://mdaudit.com/?p=29942) [VENDOR].

### Inferences
- The end-to-end flow for a typical independent group looks like this: visit → clinician documents in the EHR and picks the E/M level and diagnoses (often from EHR "pick lists") → charge drops to the practice management/billing system (possibly into a coder "charge review" work queue if the practice has coders) → claim scrubber edits → clearinghouse → payer adjudication → remittance (835) with payment, downcode or denial → denial rework. Pre-bill AI review inserts between charge drop and scrubber. It can sit in the same work queue that human coders use today, so it has to fit inside the ~1–3-day charge lag most practices target.
- Scrubbers and clearinghouses already catch format and payer-edit problems (NCCI pairs, missing modifiers). They cannot tell whether the *note supports* the level, add-on or modifier. That "documentation-to-code" gap is what CERT and payer audits penalize (Q3), and it is the gap pre-bill chart review addresses.
- Because self-coding by clinicians is the norm and audits sample ~10 charts per provider per year (~0.2–0.5% of a busy provider's visits, given the visit volumes in Q5), most errors are never seen before payment. A 100%-of-charts pre-bill model changes that coverage by more than two orders of magnitude.

### Gaps
- No recent (2020s) authoritative survey of the share of practices where clinicians self-select E/M levels versus coders. The best figure is OIG 2012 (2011 data).
- No current MGMA percentile table for charge lag by specialty (paywalled DataDive). The 3-day median is from 2019.
- No specialty-level physician-practice denial rate benchmarks that are public and current. The Experian, MGMA and Premier figures are mixed provider types.

## Q2. How E/M office visit leveling works (2021 AMA rules, 2023 extensions), why it is error-prone, typical level distributions, and the dollar difference between levels (2025–2026)

### Takeaway
Since 2021, a clinician picks an office visit level (99202–99205 new, 99212–99215 established) by **either** (a) medical decision making (MDM), where 2 of 3 elements must be met, **or** (b) total clinician time on the date of service. The rules require judgment ("moderate" vs "low" problem complexity, what counts as "data", what counts as "prescription drug management"), and documentation must show the elements explicitly. Medicare data shows a large shift toward level 4 and 5 since 2019, especially in orthopedics and psychiatry. Each step up is worth about **$36–$60 per visit at 2026 Medicare national rates** (non-facility), plus add-ons like **G2211 ($17.37)** that are captured on only ~14.5% of eligible visits.

### Cited Findings

#### The rules
- **MDM** has three elements: (1) number and complexity of problems addressed at the encounter, (2) amount and/or complexity of data reviewed and analyzed, (3) risk of complications and/or morbidity or mortality of patient management. There are four MDM levels: straightforward, low, moderate, high. "To qualify for a particular level of MDM, two of the three elements for that level of MDM must be met or exceeded." — [AMA CPT E/M guidelines, 2023 edition, p.7](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf) [AUTH].
- **Time** = total time on the date of the encounter, face-to-face and non-face-to-face, by the physician or other qualified health professional (QHP: NP/PA). It covers preparing (reviewing tests), history, exam, counseling, ordering, referring/communicating, documenting in the EHR, independently interpreting results, and care coordination. It excludes separately reported services, travel, general teaching, and clinical staff time. Total time must be documented when used to select the code — [AMA 2023 E/M guidelines, p.19–20](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf) [AUTH].
- **Time thresholds** (current CPT descriptors, "must be met or exceeded"): 99202 15 min, 99203 30, 99204 45, 99205 60. 99212 10, 99213 20, 99214 30, 99215 40. The 2026 Medicare relative value file descriptors read "Office o/p new low 30 min (99203)… est mod 30 min (99214)… est hi 40 min (99215)" — [CMS RVU26D file](https://www.cms.gov/files/zip/rvu26d-updated-08-26-2026.zip) [AUTH]. (In 2021–2023 these were ranges, e.g., 99214 = 30–39 min. CERT 2024 still labels "99214 30-39 min" — [CMS CERT 2024](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/2024-medicare-fee-service-supplemental-improper-payment-data.pdf).)
- **2023 extension**: the MDM/time framework was extended to hospital inpatient/observation (99221–99239), consultations (99242–99245, 99252–99255), emergency department (99281–99285, MDM only), nursing facility (99304–99316), and home/residence services (99341–99350). Several codes were deleted (e.g., 99241, 99251, 99217–99220, 99343) and prolonged-services codes 99354–99357 were deleted — [AMA 2023 E/M guidelines, p.1](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/2023-e-m-descriptors-guidelines.pdf) [AUTH].
- **Prolonged services differ by payer.** CPT 99417 starts at 55 min for 99215 (40 + 15). Medicare does not recognize 99417 (its 2026 status indicator is "I", invalid for Medicare) and instead pays **G2212**, which starts only when the *maximum* of the old level-5 range is exceeded by 15 minutes (≈69 min established / ≈89 min new) — [CMS RVU26D](https://www.cms.gov/files/zip/rvu26d-updated-08-26-2026.zip) [AUTH, status indicator]; thresholds per [MedLearn/ICD10monitor](https://medlearn.com/icd10monitor/prolonged-services-in-cpt-versus-medicare-they-do-not-agree/) [trade; verify against CMS MLN E/M booklet].
- **G2211** (visit complexity add-on for longitudinal care) has been paid by Medicare since 2024. CMS's **2027 proposed rule** (released Jul 14, 2026; not final) would replace G2211 with a modifier that raises the E/M payment by **16%**, or **32%** for clinicians in a Shared Savings Program ACO or LEAD model. It would also pay same-day office E/M + 0/10/90-day global procedures at 100% for the higher-valued service and **50%** for the others — [CMS CY2027 PFS proposed rule fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-proposed-rule) [AUTH].

#### Why leveling is error-prone (evidence)
- In 2025, CERT's single largest root cause for established office visit errors was "documentation supports lower level of E/M service than what was billed": **120 sampled claims**, versus 7 claims where documentation supported a *higher* level — [CMS CERT 2025, Table 7](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf) [AUTH].
- The 2001 King et al. study (600 Illinois family physicians, 6 standardized notes) found physicians agreed with expert coders about half the time on established-patient notes, mostly undercoding. On new-patient notes agreement was lower and errors were mostly overcoding. The authors blamed guideline complexity and limited training — [King, Sharp, Lipsky, J Am Board Fam Pract 2001](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/commhealth_fac/73/) [AUTH, **2001, pre-2021 rules**].
- An AAPC audit of a large hospital system reported the 2021 rules lowered coding accuracy in both directions. Many 99204/99214 visits supported 99205/99215, and many 99212s supported moderate MDM (99214) — [AAPC, Looking Back on 2021 E/M Challenges](https://www.aapc.com/blog/84470-looking-back-on-2021-e-m-changes/) [SURVEY/trade; page not opened, figure from search summary].

### Inferences
- **Leveling is where most of the money is.** The 99213→99214 step (+$40.42) and 99214→99215 step (+$56.78) are each 30–40% of the base visit payment. Getting one level wrong is a large fraction of the visit's value, in both directions.
- **The distribution data shows two different stories by specialty.** Primary care and cardiology are already concentrated at 99214 (63–75%), so their marginal risk is *overcoding at 99215* and *missed add-ons*, more than undercoding at 99213. Dermatology, orthopedics and PA-heavy practices still bill 99213 for half or more of established visits, so they are the likeliest pockets of *undercoding*. Orthopedics (+15 points at 99214 since 2019) and psychiatry (+15 points) moved fastest. That is a signal of both real undercoding correction and growing audit exposure (ortho has the highest CERT office-visit error rate, Q3).
- **Add-on capture is a pure-uplift lever with low compliance risk when documentation supports it.** With G2211 on only 14.5% of eligible Medicare visits, a primary care clinician with 1,000 eligible Medicare visits a year who moved from the PCP average (24.1%) to 60% would add about 359 × $17.37 ≈ **$6,200/yr** at national rates (illustrative arithmetic, Medicare FFS only). Under the 2027 proposal (16% of the E/M payment), the per-visit value on a 99214 would rise to ≈$21.70 (16% × $135.61, before the 2027 conversion-factor cut). A G2211 check has to be built to switch to the modifier version if it is finalized.
- **Payer-specific rules belong in the rules engine.** Medicare's G2212 versus CPT 99417 (different code *and* threshold) and Medicare's G2211 (not universally recognized by commercial payers) show the engine needs payer-aware logic, not just CPT logic.

### Gaps
- Full-population (all specialties) national 99211–99215 mix for 2025 was not computed (PSPS rows for all specialties are ~200k per code). The specialty-level figures above cover the main segments.
- Commercial-payer level distributions and commercial fee levels relative to Medicare were not found in public data.
- I could not open the Annals G2211 paper or the Epic Research page directly (paywall/403). Those figures are from summaries.
- Inter-rater agreement among certified coders on E/M levels: a figure of ~58.7% agreement on hypothetical cases surfaced in search summaries, but I could not trace it to its original study, so it is excluded. Current (post-2021) inter-coder agreement data was not found. It matters for setting an AI accuracy target, because human coders are not a perfect gold standard.

## Q3. The main error types and how often they occur (with authoritative vs. vendor data separated)

### Takeaway
In audited Medicare data (CERT 2025, claims Jul 2023–Jun 2024), **about 1 in 6 established office-visit claim lines (17.5%) had a payment error**. The dollar-weighted improper payment rate was 5.3% for established and 7.6% for new office visits. About two-thirds to three-quarters of those dollars were **incorrect coding (level too high)**, and the rest were missing or insufficient documentation. In CERT, overcoding outweighs undercoding about **7:1** by dollars. Practice-level audit studies, by contrast, usually find clinicians **undercode** more than they overcode, and add-ons like G2211 are mostly not billed. A pre-bill product therefore has to be bidirectional: it should catch unsupported high levels and modifiers (compliance) and supported-but-unbilled levels and add-ons (revenue).

### Cited Findings

- Missed add-on: G2211 captured on only 14.5% of eligible Medicare visits in 2024, and 76% of eligible clinicians never billed it — [JAMA 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12921562/) [AUTH].
- Older evidence (flagged): King et al. 2001 found mostly undercoding on established visits and overcoding on new visits — [J Am Board Fam Pract 2001](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/commhealth_fac/73/). CERT's Part B "Other" error category (5.5% of Part B improper dollars) and payer edits capture part of it — [CMS CERT 2025, Table J1](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf).

#### Vendor claims (directional only)
- Charta Health reports an average **11% revenue increase** (an "11:1 ROI"), up to 15.2% more RVUs per encounter, 10.6× more chart reviews per FTE, and "72% greater accuracy than internal teams" — [Bain Capital Ventures](https://baincapitalventures.com/insight/charting-a-new-era-how-charta-health-is-transforming-medical-chart-review/); [HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2025/07/22/charta-health-secures-22m-ai-powered-automated-chart-review/) [VENDOR, no independent validation found].

### Inferences
- **Two error populations call for two product jobs.** (1) *Compliance*: unsupported 99215/99205, unsupported modifier 25, 99211 without required elements, missing signatures/date documentation, incident-to supervision. CERT shows these concentrate the improper dollars, and payers (Cigna R49) are automating downcoding against them. (2) *Revenue*: supported-but-unbilled higher levels (mainly at 99212/99213 in derm/ortho/PA-heavy settings), unbilled G2211, unbilled prolonged time (G2212/99417), and procedures documented but not charged.
- CERT systematically under-measures (2). It only reviews what was billed, so an add-on never billed (G2211) never shows as an "underpayment". The CERT 7:1 overcoding ratio should not be read as "undercoding is rare". It means *billed* claims mostly err high, while *unbilled* revenue is invisible to CERT.
- **Ranked first set of checks** for an MVP, by evidence of frequency × dollars × buildability:
  1. E/M level support, bidirectional: MDM 2-of-3 scoring from the note, plus a time-statement check (present, ≥ threshold, excludes separately billed services). Evidence: CERT's #1 root cause, 17.5% line error rate, Utah undercoding.
  2. Add-on capture: G2211 eligibility (longitudinal relationship documented), payer-specific prolonged services (G2212 at ≥69/89 min for Medicare vs 99417 at ≥55/75 min for CPT payers). Evidence: 14.5% G2211 capture.
  3. Modifier 25 support: separately identifiable problem documented beyond the procedure's pre-service work (e.g., AWV/preventive + problem visit, derm/ortho procedures). Evidence: CERT root cause, OIG 22/24, 2027 proposed cut.
  4. Documentation completeness gates: signed and dated note for the billed DOS, rendering/supervising clinician, incident-to supervision, telehealth POS/modifier consistency (POS 10/02 + 95/93). Evidence: CERT no-doc/missing-DOS root causes.
  5. High-risk codes: 99211 (37.1% CERT rate), 99215/99205 (14.8%/11.1%). Behavioral health psychotherapy time documentation (OIG 59% failure).
  6. Diagnosis specificity and HCC capture for risk-bearing customers (Q6).

### Gaps
- No authoritative 2024–2026 national estimate of physician *undercoding* prevalence in commercial populations. Peer-reviewed evidence is small, single-site or old.
- No public data on missed-procedure charges (documented but not billed) in physician offices.
- No public prevalence data on unbundling/modifier 59 errors specific to physician offices for 2024–2026.
- A widely repeated "Florida: 9% of primary care visits undercoded, $114M lost" figure (attributed to Tenpas & Dietrich 2023) could not be traced to the original paper, so it is excluded.

## Q4. Revenue leakage estimates, and the cost and coverage of manual coding audits today (coder salaries, shortages)

### Takeaway
There is no authoritative national number for physician-practice revenue leakage. The credible evidence is (a) CERT's ~5–8% improper rate on office E/M in Medicare (mostly overcoding risk), (b) small audits showing 10–35% of E/M work RVUs left unbilled at single clinics, and (c) G2211 being billed on only ~14.5% of eligible visits. The often-quoted "3–5% of net revenue lost to charge capture" is vendor-sourced. Manual auditing is cheap per chart ($20–40 per chart anecdotally) but covers a tiny sample. Coders cost ~$50k (BLS median, medical records specialists) to ~$64.5k (AAPC average for a CPC credential), and the workforce is tight.

### Inferences
- **Illustrative leakage model (Medicare-rate arithmetic, per 1,000 established office visits, family-practice mix from PSPS 2025)**. If 10% of the 284 99213 visits actually support 99214: 28 × $40.42 ≈ $1,150. If G2211 capture moves from 24% to 60% of eligible visits: ≈$6,200. If 2% of visits qualify for unbilled prolonged time at G2212 ($34.07): ≈$680. Total ≈ **$8,000 per 1,000 visits ≈ 6–7% of the ~$124 average established-visit payment** for that mix. Against that, the compliance downside from CERT-like overcoding is 3.1% of established-visit dollars at risk. These are assumptions applied to authoritative prices, not measured outcomes. They are in the same order of magnitude as the vendor-claimed 3–11%.
- **Manual audit economics**: 10 charts/provider/year at $20–$40/chart is $200–$400 per provider per year, which is cheap but covers ≈0.25% of visits for a clinician seeing ~4,000/yr. Reviewing 100% of charts manually at even $5/chart would cost ~$20,000 per provider per year, more than most leakage estimates recover. That cost gap is why "100% pre-bill" was not feasible before AI. A coder at $50–65k/yr (BLS–AAPC), reviewing perhaps 15–25 office charts per hour, can cover tens of thousands of charts a year *if* they only review flagged charts. This suggests the MVP should be an *exception router* for existing coders/billers, not a replacement.

### Gaps
- No authoritative, current physician-practice revenue leakage benchmark (MGMA/HFMA primary reports not found publicly).
- AAPC 2024 and 2025 salary survey figures were not retrievable (secondary sites cite ~$64.7–64.9k for 2024, unverified).
- Productivity benchmarks for pro-fee coders (charts per hour for office E/M) were not found from an authoritative source.
- Offshore coding cost per chart was not found from a non-vendor source.

## Q5. Segment comparison (primary care, urgent care, FQHCs, behavioral health, dermatology, orthopedics, home health, multi-specialty/MSOs, PE-backed roll-ups) and wedge recommendation

### Takeaway
Recommended wedges: **(1) independent and MSO/PE-backed primary care groups (including NP/PA-heavy practices) with meaningful Medicare/Medicare Advantage mix**, and **(2) multi-site urgent care operators**. Both have high visit volume, a narrow and standardized code set (office E/M plus a short list of add-ons, modifiers and procedures), fee-for-service payment where coding changes dollars, centralized billing teams that will buy an exception-routing tool, and fast decisions. Primary care adds a second value stream (G2211, prolonged time, AWV + problem visit with modifier 25, and HCC capture for risk contracts). Dermatology and orthopedics are strong *second* segments (modifier 25 and procedure logic, highest CERT office-visit error rate in ortho, PE roll-up buyers) but need procedure/global-period rules. **Avoid at first**: FQHCs (per-visit PPS payment means E/M level rarely changes revenue), home health (institutional PDGM claims, different product), and large multi-specialty/academic groups (long Epic-centric sales cycles, rule breadth).

### Cited Findings

#### Market structure and buyers
- 2024 AMA Physician Practice Benchmark: 42.2% of physicians work in physician-owned private practices (down from 60.1% in 2012). 34.5% in hospital-owned practices. **6.5% in private-equity-owned practices** (up from ~4.5% in 2020/2022). Ophthalmology has the highest private-practice share (70.4%) and cardiology the lowest (30.7%) — [Medical Economics summary of AMA benchmark](https://www.medicaleconomics.com/view/independent-practice-losing-ground-to-hospital-corporate-private-equity-ownership); [AMA Physician Practice Benchmark Survey page](https://www.ama-assn.org/about/research/physician-practice-benchmark-survey) [AUTH-ish: AMA survey].
- Ambulatory EHR installed base (Definitive Healthcare): Epic 19.5% (16,295 installs), eClinicalWorks 11.9% (9,966), athenahealth 6.9% (5,801), NextGen 4.2% (3,510), ModMed 3.6% (2,987) — [Definitive Healthcare](https://www.definitivehc.com/blog/top-ambulatory-ehr-systems) [industry data; methodology not disclosed]. Charta is distributed via the athenahealth Marketplace (~160,000 providers) — [HIT Consultant](https://hitconsultant.net/2025/07/22/charta-health-secures-22m-ai-powered-automated-chart-review/) [VENDOR].

#### Primary care (family practice, internal medicine, NP/PA-led)
- Volume: Medicare FFS 2025 established office E/M services: FP 25.7M, IM 24.9M, NPs 31.4M, PAs 18.0M (NP/PA span all specialties) — [DERIVED, PSPS 2025](https://data.cms.gov/data-api/v1/dataset/aa28896d-e502-4742-8b8d-65177ff45c7a/data).
- Mix: ~90% of established visits at 99213/99214. 99214 is 63–64% for FP/IM, and 99215 is 4.9–7.5%. Modifier 25 is on ~24–26% of E/M lines (often a problem visit billed with a preventive/AWV service) — [DERIVED, PSPS 2025](https://data.cms.gov/data-api/v1/dataset/aa28896d-e502-4742-8b8d-65177ff45c7a/data).
- Errors: CERT 2025 established office visit improper rate IM 5.7% (largest dollars, $156M), FP 2.0%, NP 4.1%, PA 6.0% — [CMS CERT 2025 Table H3](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nov-2025-medicare-ffs-supplemental-improper-payment-data-2025922.pdf). G2211 on 24.1% of PCP-eligible visits — [JAMA 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12921562/).
- Payment: FFS (commercial + traditional Medicare) plus growing risk-based contracts. 54% of Medicare beneficiaries were in Medicare Advantage in 2025 (34.1M) — [Becker's summary of KFF](https://www.beckerspayer.com/payer/the-state-of-medicare-advantage-enrollment-in-2025-6-notes/) [secondary of KFF]. MA pay depends on diagnosis-based risk scores (Q6).

#### Urgent care
- Volume: Medicare FFS POS 20 (urgent care facility) 2025: 3.74M established + 1.63M new office E/M services. 99213 53.5% / 99214 41.7% (est.). 99203 52.0% / 99204 43.0% (new) — [DERIVED, PSPS 2025](https://data.cms.gov/data-api/v1/dataset/aa28896d-e502-4742-8b8d-65177ff45c7a/data). The UCA's own benchmarking report is paywalled — [UCA](https://urgentcareassociation.org/?p=15344).

#### FQHCs (federally qualified health centers)

### Inferences
- **Why primary care first:** (1) the largest volume of the most standardized codes, so one rule pack (99202–99215 MDM/time, G2211, G2212/99417, AWV/IPPE + modifier 25, telehealth POS/modifiers) covers most dollars. (2) Bidirectional value: undercoding/add-on capture (G2211 at 24% of eligible PCP visits) plus compliance (CERT IM 5.7%, rising 99215 share, payer algorithmic downcoding such as Cigna R49). (3) A second revenue line in risk contracts (HCC capture, Q6) on the same notes. (4) Buyers in MSOs and PE-backed groups are ROI-driven. Risk: Charta's athenahealth Marketplace presence means athena-based primary care is contested. An entrant may find white space on eClinicalWorks/NextGen or among Epic Community Connect users (inference; see the EHR integration notes from the parallel researcher).
- **Why urgent care second:** a narrow code set (mostly 99203/99204/99213/99214 plus a short procedure list), very high volume per site, same-day billing pressure (so a fast pre-bill turnaround is valued), and centralized multi-site billing. Medicare data shows urgent care bills 99214 less often than primary care (41.7% vs 63.8%), so undercoding opportunity is plausible but unproven. Validate with commercial data in pilots.
- **Why not FQHCs first:** with Medicare and Medicaid paying a flat per-visit rate (≈60% of patients are Medicaid/Medicare/dual), moving a visit from 99213 to 99214 usually doesn't change payment for those payers. Value would come from *qualifying-visit* rules, commercial claims, UDS/quality reporting, and HCC capture for MA/ACO. These are a different product emphasis, sold to price-sensitive buyers.
- **Why not home health:** it is a different claim type (institutional), different coders, and a different rule system (PDGM/OASIS). CERT shows its errors are documentation and medical necessity rather than coding. That is a separate product.
- **Derm and ortho as the expansion path:** both are heavily PE-consolidated and procedure-dense. Ortho has the highest CERT office-visit error rate and the steepest level-4 growth, and derm's 61% modifier-25 rate faces a proposed 2027 payment cut. Pre-bill modifier-25 validation is a compliance story these buyers will feel in 2027.

## Q6. The value-based care angle: HCC risk adjustment capture for risk-bearing providers, and how big it is compared with fee-for-service coding uplift

### Takeaway
For providers in Medicare Advantage capitation or ACO risk contracts, the money is in **documenting and coding chronic conditions accurately** (HCCs), not in visit levels. Payment per patient scales with the risk score. MedPAC projects MA payments of about **$16,242 per enrollee in 2026**, so a 0.1 change in average risk score on a panel is worth on the order of ~$1,000–1,600 per patient per year to the plan (before contract splits). That is far larger per patient than E/M uplift (a few dollars to tens of dollars per visit). But the policy direction is tightening: the V28 model (fully in effect for 2026) removed many codes, CMS will stop counting diagnoses from "unlinked" chart reviews in 2027, and coding intensity is under heavy scrutiny. A pre-bill product can add HCC "suspect" and specificity checks as a second module for risk-bearing primary care customers. It should be positioned as *accuracy and documentation support*, not "capture more".

### Cited Findings
- **Enrollment**: 34.1M (54%) of ~62.8M eligible Medicare beneficiaries were in MA in 2025 — [Becker's summary of KFF (Jul 2025)](https://www.beckerspayer.com/payer/the-state-of-medicare-advantage-enrollment-in-2025-6-notes/) [secondary of KFF].
- **Payments**: MedPAC (Mar 2026) expects MA payments to average **$16,242 per enrollee in 2026** (including $2,660 in rebates). It estimates MA spending ~14% (≈$76B) above what FFS would spend for the same people. 2025 payments to plans were ~$537B for ~34.9M enrollees — [Becker's Hospital Review](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/finance/medicare-advantage-spending-14-higher-than-fee-for-service-report/); [MedPAC March 2026 press release](https://www.medpac.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/March_2026_MedPAC_Report_Press_Release_SEC.pdf) [AUTH via secondary].
- **Coding intensity**: MedPAC estimates MA coding intensity of about 4% (≈$28B) in 2026 after V28 and the statutory 5.9% minimum coding-pattern adjustment, down from ~10% in 2022–2023. Chart reviews add codes for 1 in 6 MA enrollees that don't appear on any encounter. Starting with 2027 payments, CMS excludes diagnoses from **unlinked chart reviews** (not tied to an encounter), an estimated 1.5% payment reduction. V28 was phased in over 2024–2026 — [KFF, Decoding MA Coding Intensity (Jul 1, 2026)](https://www.kff.org/medicare/decoding-medicare-advantage-coding-intensity/) [AUTH-quality policy analysis]. MedPAC's 2025 estimate was ~$84B excess, with ~16% higher MA risk scores before adjustment (≈$40B from coding) — [Becker's Payer](https://www.beckerspayer.com/payer/medpac-estimates-84b-in-medicare-advantage-overpayments-in-2025-10-notes/) [secondary].
- **V28 mechanics**: a single HCC's dollar value depends on the plan's county benchmark and normalization. For example, the three V28 dementia HCCs (125/126/127) each carry a coefficient of 0.341 — [Creyos](https://creyos.com/blog/v28-medicare-advantage) [VENDOR]. V28 removed 2,027 ICD-10 codes from payment HCCs, and America's Physician Groups estimated 17%+ revenue loss for some organizations — per [Wolters Kluwer](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/how-cms-hcc-version-28-will-impact-risk-adjustment-factor-raf-scores) [industry; attribution via search summary].
- **Vendor case study**: a 12,000-life MA primary care panel reportedly moved average RAF from 0.90 to 1.00 and chronic recapture from 71% to 96% (+11% PMPM) — [ASPRCM case study](https://asprcmsolutions.com/case-studies/hcc-v28-raf-recapture/) [VENDOR, no methodology].
- **ACO linkage**: the 2027 proposed rule would give Shared Savings Program ACO / LEAD clinicians a 32% E/M add-on modifier (vs 16% otherwise), with claims counting toward ACO benchmarks — [CMS CY2027 fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/calendar-year-cy-2027-medicare-physician-fee-schedule-proposed-rule) [AUTH, proposed].

### Inferences
- **Size comparison (illustrative)**: for a 1,000-patient MA panel, raising average RAF by 0.05 through accurate documentation of conditions that are truly present and managed is worth ≈0.05 × ~$16k ≈ $800/patient/yr, or ~$0.8M/yr to the plan, of which a risk-bearing group may keep 50–85% depending on contract (assumption). The FFS E/M uplift on the same patients (say ~4 visits/yr × ~$8 of net uplift per visit from Q4) is ≈$32/patient/yr. **HCC accuracy is ~10–25× larger per patient** for groups that are actually in full-risk MA contracts. For groups paid FFS only, HCC capture is worth little directly (only via MSSP benchmarks and quality).
- **But the risk and regulatory profile is different.** OIG, DOJ and MedPAC focus on MA coding intensity. The 2027 unlinked-chart-review exclusion pushes value toward *encounter-linked, pre-bill* documentation of conditions actually addressed at the visit, which suits a pre-bill product. The checks should be "documentation supports a more specific diagnosis code that was addressed (MEAT: monitored, evaluated, assessed, treated)" and "condition listed in the problem list but not addressed or coded" prompts to the clinician, not retrospective add-only chart mining.
- **Product sequencing**: start with FFS pro-fee checks (fast, demonstrable ROI on every claim). Add an HCC/diagnosis-specificity module for primary care customers with MA risk or ACO contracts as the upsell. This also lets the primary care wedge stand alone if a customer has no risk contracts.

### Gaps
- No authoritative per-HCC dollar value. It depends on county benchmarks, normalization, the coding-pattern adjustment and contract terms.
- No public data on how much MA revenue risk-bearing *physician groups* (versus plans) retain from RAF changes.
- The share of primary care practices in two-sided risk contracts was not retrieved (e.g., HCP-LAN APM measurement), so the size of the HCC-relevant customer base is unquantified here.
