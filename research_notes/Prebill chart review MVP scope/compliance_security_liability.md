# Compliance, Security, and Legal-Liability Requirements for a Pre-Bill AI Chart Review / AI Coding Startup (US outpatient)

Research date: 2026-10-21. US only. All dollar figures are estimates; vendor-published pricing is flagged as such. "Unsettled" means courts or regulators have not clearly decided the question.

Plain-language glossary used throughout:
- **PHI (protected health information):** any patient-identifiable health or billing information (a chart note, a claim, a diagnosis code tied to a patient).
- **Covered entity (CE):** the provider group, health system, or health plan that bills or pays for care and is directly regulated by HIPAA.
- **Business associate (BA):** an outside company that handles PHI to do a job *for* a covered entity (e.g., a billing or coding vendor). A **BAA** is the contract HIPAA requires between them.
- **Subcontractor BA:** a vendor's own vendor that touches PHI (cloud host, LLM API provider).
- **False Claims Act (FCA):** federal law that makes it illegal to knowingly submit, or cause someone else to submit, a false bill to Medicare/Medicaid/other federal programs; penalties are triple damages plus per-claim fines. **Qui tam / relator:** a whistleblower who sues on the government's behalf and gets a share.
- **Upcoding:** billing a higher-paying code than the documentation supports. **Downcoding:** a payer paying at a lower level than billed.
- **E/M codes:** "evaluation and management" codes for office visits (e.g., 99212-99215 for established patients); levels 4-5 pay more and are the main target of payer audits.
- **Risk adjustment / HCC coding (Medicare Advantage):** diagnoses on claims raise a patient's "risk score," which increases what Medicare pays the health plan.

## 1. HIPAA: business associate status, BAA obligations, minimum necessary, subcontractors, breach notification, and practical program setup

### Takeaway
A pre-bill coding product that reads charts for a provider is a HIPAA business associate by regulatory definition (billing, claims processing, data analysis, and quality assurance are all named BA functions), so it must sign BAAs with every customer, flow down BAAs to every vendor that touches PHI (cloud, LLM API, logging/observability), follow "minimum necessary," and notify customers of breaches within 60 days. The proposed HIPAA Security Rule overhaul (Jan 2025 NPRM) is still not final as of Oct 2026, but buyers already expect its controls (MFA, encryption, asset inventory) in questionnaires.

### Cited Findings
- **BA definition squarely covers coding/billing vendors:** a business associate is anyone who, on behalf of a covered entity (not as its employee), "creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information for a function or activity" such as "claims processing or administration, data analysis," "utilization review, quality assurance," and billing. — [45 CFR 160.103 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)
- **Subcontractors are BAs too:** the definition includes "A subcontractor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits protected health information" "on behalf of the business associate"; a subcontractor is anyone to whom a BA delegates a function involving PHI. — [45 CFR 160.103 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/160.103)
- **Flow-down BAAs:** "A business associate may disclose protected health information to a business associate that is a subcontractor" only after obtaining "satisfactory assurances" (i.e., a subcontractor BAA). — [45 CFR 164.502(e) (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)
- **Minimum necessary applies directly to BAs:** "a covered entity or business associate must make reasonable efforts to limit" PHI used, disclosed, or requested to the minimum necessary. — [45 CFR 164.502(b) (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.502)
- **Breach notification by a BA:** the BA must "notify the covered entity" of a breach of unsecured PHI "without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery," identifying each affected individual to the extent possible and supplying other information the CE needs to notify patients. (The CE then handles patient/HHS/media notice.) — [45 CFR 164.410 (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/164.410)
- **Cloud providers (2016 OCR guidance):** a CE or BA may use a cloud service provider (CSP) for ePHI if it signs a HIPAA-compliant BAA; a CSP that is a subcontractor to a BA is itself a BA; and the CSP remains a BA "even if the CSP processes or stores only encrypted ePHI and lacks an encryption key." CSPs are contractually liable under the BAA and directly liable for applicable HIPAA requirements. — [Mintz summary of HHS cloud guidance (Oct 2016)](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2016-10-19-hhs-publishes-guidance-hipaa-and-cloud-computing); [copy of HHS cloud computing guidance](https://facesandvoicesofrecovery.org/wp-content/uploads/Documents/Guidance_HHS_Cloud-Computing.pdf)
- **No OCR guidance specific to LLM vendors was found;** the cloud guidance logic is the operative analogy. — (search finding; see Gaps)
- **OpenAI:** BAAs cover the API only for organizations approved for zero data retention (ZDR) and only on ZDR-eligible endpoints; ZDR means customer content is not logged for human review and not saved to disk. Requests go to baa@openai.com and are reviewed case by case. — [OpenAI BAA/HIPAA guide](https://cdn.openai.com/osa/baa-hipaa-guide.pdf); [OpenAI HIPAA guide](https://cdn.openai.com/osa/hipaa-guide.pdf)
- **Anthropic:** offers BAAs for "HIPAA-ready" services (first-party API with a defined feature subset and sales-assisted Enterprise plans), not consumer/Team plans or Console/Workbench; one secondary source says Batch API, Files API, Code Execution, and Computer Use are excluded and reports a June 2026 policy requiring 30-day retention for certain models even under ZDR (single secondary source; verify). — [IntuitionLabs guide (secondary)](https://intuitionlabs.ai/insights/guides/zero-data-retention-claude-anthropic-life-sciences)
- **AWS Bedrock / Azure OpenAI:** AWS's BAA is accepted via AWS Artifact and PHI may be processed only in HIPAA-eligible services; Azure OpenAI's BAA comes through Microsoft's product terms/DPA. — [Paubox AI HIPAA toolkit (secondary)](https://www.paubox.com/hubfs/gated-assets/ai-healthcare-toolkit/ai-healthcare-hipaa-toolkit.pdf?hsLang=en)
- **Security Rule overhaul status:** NPRM published Jan 6, 2025; the Unified Agenda moved final action from May 2026 to July 2027; the current (unchanged) Security Rule is what OCR enforces today; 100+ provider groups asked HHS to withdraw it. — [Davis Wright Tremaine (July 2026)](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/07/hhs-updates-hipaa-rulemaking-timeframes); [Pact-One (Sept 2026)](https://www.pact-one.com/2026/09/hipaa-security-rule-2026-status/)
- **What the proposal would change (if finalized):** eliminates "required vs. addressable" distinction; mandates encryption and MFA, network segmentation, anti-malware, vulnerability scanning at least every six months; adds direct BA verification obligations to covered entities; ~60 days to effectiveness plus 180-day compliance window. — [Pact-One](https://www.pact-one.com/2026/09/hipaa-security-rule-2026-status/); [HIPAA Journal on BA impact](https://www.hipaajournal.com/hipaa-security-rule-business-associates/)

### Inferences
- **The startup is a BA from day one of any pilot using real charts**, even if the pilot is free and even if data is encrypted. There is no "pilot exemption." Using fully de-identified data is the only way to avoid BA status, and de-identified data is rarely practical for a coding product that must tie suggestions to specific claims.
- **Every LLM call that carries chart text is a PHI disclosure to a subcontractor BA.** Practically: use only BAA-covered configurations (e.g., Bedrock/Azure/Vertex under the cloud BAA, or a direct API BAA with ZDR), disable features excluded from the BAA, and confirm data-retention terms in writing. Observability/logging tools (Datadog, Sentry, LangSmith, etc.) that capture prompts also become subcontractor BAs or must be configured to scrub PHI.
- **Minimum necessary in product design:** pull only the encounter documents and claim fields needed for the coding decision, not full patient histories, unless prior-encounter context is genuinely needed (e.g., chronic condition support); document that rationale.
- **Practical startup HIPAA program (based on Security/Privacy Rule structure):** named Privacy and Security Officer (can be founders), documented security risk analysis, written policies (access control, encryption, incident response, breach notification, workforce sanctions, data retention/destruction), annual workforce HIPAA training, a BAA template plus subcontractor BAA inventory, audit logging of PHI access, and a vendor inventory. Compliance-automation platforms (Vanta/Drata/Secureframe) bundle HIPAA policy templates and control tracking alongside SOC 2 (see Section 2), so the incremental cost of HIPAA readiness is mostly staff time if a platform is already purchased.
- **Downstream contracts:** customers' BAAs will often be stricter than the HIPAA minimum (e.g., 24-72 hour breach notice instead of 60 days, cyber-insurance requirements, offshore-data restrictions, no training on customer data). Expect to negotiate these; plan to prohibit training foundation models on customer PHI unless explicitly permitted.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch HHS.gov pages directly (403); HHS BA direct-liability fact sheet and current civil money penalty tiers were not verified this session.
- No OCR guidance specifically addressing LLM/genAI vendors, prompt logs, or model training on PHI was found.
- Anthropic's retention terms are from a single secondary source; verify against Anthropic's current BAA documentation.

## 2. Security certifications and assurances buyers expect (SOC 2, HITRUST, questionnaires, cyber insurance)

### Takeaway
For early pilots with independent outpatient groups, a signed BAA, a security questionnaire, a pen test, and a SOC 2 Type I (or "in progress" Type II) is usually enough; health systems and payers typically expect SOC 2 Type II and increasingly HITRUST (i1 or r2), plus Tech E&O/cyber insurance at $5M+ limits. Budget roughly $25K-$60K for a first-year SOC 2 Type II (platform + auditor + pen test), and $70K-$200K+ for HITRUST i1/r2 later.

### Cited Findings
**SOC 2 (plain language):** an independent CPA firm's report on a company's security controls. **Type I** = controls are designed properly at a single point in time. **Type II** = controls actually operated over an observation window (typically 3-12 months). Type II costs more because it covers a period. — [SecureLeap tools comparison](https://www.secureleap.tech/blog/soc-2-tools-vanta-drata-secureframe-guide-2025); [Drata SOC 2 cost guide](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/cost)
- Auditor fees: Drata says $12,000-$20,000 for a small-to-midsize company Type II audit. — [Drata](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/cost)
- An auditor directory reports a Q3 2026 median of ~$9,000 for Type II for 1-10 employee companies, with individual firms quoting ranges like Type I $6.8K-$13.5K / Type II $9.6K-$16.3K, and larger firms Type I $15K-$45K / Type II $20K-$60K. — [SOC2Auditors.org (Oct 2026)](https://soc2auditors.org/soc-2-auditors-startups/)
- Another 2026 guide: Type I $8,000-$25,000; Type II $15,000-$40,000 for a six-month window. — [Transient Labs (vendor blog)](https://transientlabs.ai/blog/soc2-compliance-cost-2026)
- Compliance-automation platforms: Drata entry tier ~$7,500-$15,000/yr; Vanta entry ~$10,000-$15,000/yr; neither publishes list prices. — [SOC2Auditors.org software guide](https://soc2auditors.org/insights/best-soc-2-software-startups/); [UnderDefense on Vanta pricing](https://underdefense.com/blog/vanta-pricing/)
- All-in first-year totals range from ~$25,000-$50,000 (Drata's estimate) to $60,000-$95,000 for a 50-person SaaS company (vendor-affiliated guide). — [Drata](https://drata.com/learn/soc-2/cost); [The Sector Post](https://www.thesectorpost.com/compliance/soc2/audit-costs)

**HITRUST (plain language):** a healthcare-specific security certification run by the HITRUST Alliance; three levels: **e1** (essentials, ~44 controls, 1-year), **i1** (implemented, 1-year), **r2** (risk-based, 2-year, most rigorous). Health systems and payers often prefer it over SOC 2.
- Timelines: e1 ~3-6 months, i1 ~5-12 months, r2 ~8-18 months (BD Emerson, updated Oct 2026: e1 3-6, i1 5-10, r2 8-15 months, remediation drives the variation). — [BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/hitrust-certification); [Integral Healthcare Solutions](https://www.integralhs.com/hitrust-cybersecurity-cost-guide); [Security Compliance Guide](https://securitycomplianceguide.com/blog/hitrust-certification-guide/)
- Costs: external assessor alone e1 $15K-$40K, i1 $35K-$80K, r2 $70K-$200K (BD Emerson); all-in e1 ~$35K-$50K, i1 ~$70K-$120K, r2 ~$100K-$500K+ (Integral HS); Wikipedia cites ~$50K e1, ~$120K i1, $500K+ r2. — [BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/hitrust-certification); [Integral HS](https://www.integralhs.com/hitrust-cybersecurity-cost-guide); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HITRUST)
- r2 requires an interim review mid-cycle (~25-35% of original assessment fee per one estimate). — [Sprinto](https://sprinto.com/blog/hitrust-certification-cost/) (vendor blog; figure is a single estimate)
- Version caveat: one source reports a Sept 24, 2026 HITRUST advisory closing v11.8 to new e1/i1 assessments at end of 2026; not confirmed elsewhere. — [BD Emerson](https://www.bdemerson.com/article/hitrust-certification)

**Who demands what:**
- One vendor estimate says over 80% of hospitals, health systems, and health plans ask vendors for HITRUST; another says large health systems and major payers increasingly demand r2 while commercial SaaS buyers accept SOC 2 Type II; most B2B vendors start with SOC 2 and add HITRUST when healthcare growth or mandates justify it; i1 is described as the most common choice for healthcare SaaS/business associates. — [Bright Defense](https://www.brightdefense.com/resources/hitrust-vs-soc-2/); [Thoropass](https://www.thoropass.com/blog/hitrust-vs-soc-2); [AccountableHQ](https://www.accountablehq.com/post/hitrust-vs-soc-2-what-s-the-difference-and-which-is-right-for-you) (all vendor-published; treat percentages as marketing estimates)
- Vendor security reviews typically ask for SOC 2, HIPAA controls, and proof of cyber coverage; most MSAs require proof of Tech E&O and Cyber. — [Corgi health-tech insurance page (insurer marketing)](https://corgi.insure/health-tech)

**Pen testing:** SaaS/web app pen tests typically $5,000-$30,000; cheap "starting from" offers may be little more than automated scans. — [Astra (vendor)](https://www.getastra.com/blog/penetration-testing/cost/); [Blaze InfoSec (vendor)](https://www.blazeinfosec.com/post/how-much-does-penetration-testing-cost/)

**Insurance:**
- Example hospital requirement: combined Technology E&O and Cyber policy with minimum $5,000,000 each claim / $5,000,000 aggregate, covering PHI, privacy regulatory fines, and network security liability. — [Houston Methodist IT Insurance Requirements](https://it.houstonmethodist.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/IT-Insurance-Requirements.pdf)
- An insurer's marketing page says hospital systems require $5M-$10M Tech E&O and Cyber limits before onboarding. — [Corgi health-tech](https://corgi.insure/health-tech)
- General tech E&O benchmarks (not health-specific): ~$67-$110/month average for $1M/$1M; SaaS startup ~$929/yr; 56% of Insureon customers pick $1M/$1M. — [Insureon](https://it.insureon.com/small-business-insurance/eo/cost); [TechInsurance](https://techinsurance.com/technology-business-insurance/cost); [MoneyGeek](https://www.moneygeek.com/insurance/business/tech-it/errors-and-omissions/)
- Embroker data: 95% of enterprise software and health care technology companies bought the lowest limit in 2024. — [Insurance Edge on Embroker (Mar 2025)](https://insurance-edge.net/2025/03/13/embroker-looks-at-tech-companies-risk-management/)

### Inferences
- **Sequencing by buyer type:**
  - *Independent outpatient groups (small/mid practices):* BAA + short questionnaire + evidence of HIPAA program; SOC 2 Type I or "Type II in observation" is usually sufficient; $1M-$2M cyber/E&O may suffice.
  - *Health systems / large MSOs / academic medical centers:* formal third-party risk review (long questionnaire, often via a vendor-risk platform), SOC 2 Type II required, HITRUST often preferred, $5M+ combined Tech E&O/cyber, pen test letter, often architecture/data-flow review including LLM subprocessors.
  - *Payers (if ever selling to them):* HITRUST r2 frequently expected.
- **Insurance cost reality:** published premium benchmarks are for $1M generic tech policies; a health-tech company handling PHI and seeking $5M combined Tech E&O + cyber will pay materially more (likely low five figures annually at seed stage, but no source found; get broker quotes). Make sure the Tech E&O policy explicitly covers regulatory defense and that the policy does not exclude billing/coding errors or FCA-related claims (many E&O policies exclude fines/penalties and fraud).
- **SOC 2 scope tip:** include the "Confidentiality" and "Privacy" criteria or map HIPAA controls in the same platform to reuse evidence; healthcare buyers often ask for a HIPAA mapping or "SOC 2 + HIPAA" report.

- No reliable premium data for a health-tech startup at $5M Tech E&O + cyber limits.
- Standard healthcare security questionnaires (e.g., SIG, CAIQ, health-system custom questionnaires, vendor-risk platforms) were not researched in detail.

## 3. False Claims Act and fraud/abuse exposure for AI coding

### Takeaway
The billing provider remains legally responsible for every claim, but vendors are not immune: the FCA reaches anyone who "causes" a false claim (EHR vendors have paid nine-figure settlements; a coding-vendor CEO personally paid $2M), and enforcement is intensifying (record recoveries, a new DOJ fraud division, data-mining whistleblowers, OIG naming AI prompts as a risk). Autonomous auto-apply coding concentrates risk on the vendor and replicates any error across thousands of claims (the UCHealth automated-rule case is the closest precedent); "suggest" modes are safer only if the human review is meaningful. Percentage-of-recovered-revenue pricing is not illegal per se but has been an OIG red flag since 1998 and featured in the DxID case.

### Cited Findings
**Core legal standard**
- FCA "knowingly" includes deliberate indifference and reckless disregard, not only actual knowledge (31 U.S.C. § 3729(b)(1)(A)); penalties include treble damages and per-claim civil penalties. — [Tucker Ellis (June 2025)](https://www.tuckerellis.com/alerts/avoiding-false-claims-act-landmines-in-ai-assisted-coding-and-medical-billing/)
- Providers remain responsible: hospitals are "legally responsible for confirming that each claim is clinically warranted and properly documented"; CMS guidance says "accountability for coding accuracy does not change just because a hospital system uses AI"; choosing a trusted vendor is "not necessarily a safety valve." — [Tucker Ellis](https://www.tuckerellis.com/alerts/avoiding-false-claims-act-landmines-in-ai-assisted-coding-and-medical-billing/)
- "'The AI suggested it' is not a fantastic defense"; a hypothetical coder accepting ~85% of suggestions at ~0.5 seconds per chart invites the argument that no meaningful review occurred; vendor contracts "typically don't, even on paper, absorb your FCA risk" and "often heavily disclaim and limit their liability on compliance issues"; AI tools can be "one-way revenue engines that scan charts for undercoding opportunities." — [Health Law Attorney Blog (Mar 25, 2026)](https://www.healthlawattorneyblog.com/ai-is-billing-your-patients-who-holds-the-bag-the-rise-of-ambient-billing-and-coding-tools-and-the-legal-minefield-your-practice-is-walking-into/)

**Key cases (most relevant first)**
- **UCHealth, $23M (Nov 12, 2024) - automated coding rule:** from Nov 2017-Mar 2021 UCHealth hospitals automatically coded ED visits as CPT 99285 (highest level) whenever vital signs were checked more times than the hours the patient was in the ED (exception for stays <60 min). The relator was an in-house certified coding specialist; CMS had flagged UCHealth as a "high outlier"; no admission of liability; OIG reserved exclusion rights rather than imposing a Corporate Integrity Agreement. — [DOJ press release](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/uchealth-agrees-pay-23m-resolve-allegations-fraudulent-billing-emergency-department-visits); [Settlement agreement](https://www.justice.gov/media/1376721/dl); [HHS-OIG](https://oig.hhs.gov/fraud/enforcement/uchealth-agrees-to-pay-23m-to-resolve-allegations-of-fraudulent-billing-for-emergency-department-visits); [Arnold & Porter commentary (Nov 2024)](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/fca-qui-notes/posts/2024/11/beware-of-automated-or-ai-generated-billing-coding-to-government-healthcare-programs)

A newsletter linked this case to an AI tool, but that link is not confirmed by DOJ/trade coverage. — [Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/hospitals/villages-health-reaches-541m-false-claims-act-settlement-doj); [Healthcare Dive](https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/the-villages-health-medicare-overbilling-settlement-doj-humana/828915/); [WilmerHale (Aug 31, 2026)](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260831-recent-doj-settlements-highlight-false-claims-act-scrutiny-of-medicare-advantage-providers); unverified AI link: [FixHealth newsletter](https://www.fixhealth.ai/p/doj-just-dropped-a-541m-ai-upcoding)
- **United States v. Anthem (SDNY, No. 20 Civ. 2593) - one-way chart review:** DOJ alleges Anthem's retrospective chart review added codes but "knowingly failed to delete" invalid codes it found (2014-2018); still in discovery as of June 2026 (fact discovery close June 30, 2026; expert discovery March 2027); government says CMS already recovered $300M+. — [Mintz (Jan 13, 2026)](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2406/2026-01-13-medicare-advantage-under-microscope-enforcement); [Georgetown Litigation Tracker filing (June 22, 2026)](https://litigationtracker.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/United-States-v.-Anthem_2026.06.22_PLAINTIFFS-RESPONSE-TO-NOTICE.pdf)

  - Risk areas include upcoding and "Computer software programs that encourage billing personnel to enter data in fields indicating services were rendered though not actually performed or documented."

**Contingency / percentage pricing**
- Percentage-based billing arrangements are not illegal per se, but OIG views them as increasing upcoding risk; companies are advised to have robust compliance programs. — [Liles Parker (2022)](https://www.lilesparker.com/2022/07/29/changes-in-federal-state-law-and-billing-contracts/); [R1 RCM SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1472595/000119312522115151/d255437ddefm14a.htm)

### Inferences
- **"Suggest" vs. "autonomous/auto-apply" risk ladder (plain language):**
  1. *Flag-only / suggest with coder approval:* the provider's coder makes the decision; vendor exposure is mainly contract/E&O and reputational, unless the tool is designed or marketed to push revenue up (then "causing" liability becomes arguable, as in Practice Fusion). Risk rises if acceptance rates are near 100% with seconds-per-chart review (rubber-stamping).
  2. *Auto-apply with sampling QA:* the tool is effectively the coder; any systematic logic flaw replicates across every claim, exactly the UCHealth fact pattern (a single automated rule, flagged by CMS outlier analytics, discovered by an internal coder-whistleblower). Vendor becomes a much more plausible FCA co-defendant and a target for indemnity claims.
  3. *Auto-apply that adds diagnoses or amends the chart:* highest risk; overlaps with The Villages (amended records not approved by rendering providers), Kaiser (adding diagnoses not addressed), and OIG's Feb 2026 warning about AI prompts adding diagnoses.
- **For an MVP, the defensible design is "suggest, with evidence, both directions":** every suggested change cites the exact chart text supporting it; the tool flags overcoding (downward corrections) as readily as undercoding; changes affecting documentation require the rendering clinician's attestation; auto-apply limited to low-risk, deterministic edits (e.g., modifier/NCCI bundling fixes, missing required data) with customer opt-in and monitoring.
- **Bi-directionality is now a legal requirement in substance, not just an ethics choice:** one-way chart review is the core theory in Anthem, Cigna ($172M), and DaVita ($270M). A product that only finds "missed revenue" will look like a one-way program.
- **Contingency pricing:** a % of "found revenue" fee is the specific structure OIG has flagged since 1998 and was present in DxID. Lower-risk alternatives: flat per-chart/per-provider SaaS fees; if outcome-based, base it on *net accuracy* (both up and down corrections) or on denial reduction rather than incremental revenue; cap fees; carve out or price differently for federal payers; document fair-market value. AKS risk is lower than FCA risk for a pure coding vendor (no referrals/marketing), but the contingency structure is evidence a prosecutor or relator can use to argue motive/scienter.
- **Vendor liability allocation in practice:** expect customers to ask for (a) indemnity for losses caused by the vendor's errors or breaches, (b) audit-support commitments (supplying rationale/evidence when a payer audits), (c) repayment of fees on claims later found erroneous, and (d) insurance certificates. Startups typically cap liability at 12 months of fees with a higher "super-cap" for data breaches; regulatory fines and FCA penalties are commonly excluded from indemnity and from insurance. (Market-practice inference; see Gaps.)
- **Provider's 60-day overpayment duty:** once the customer learns of overpayments (e.g., because the tool's QA uncovers systematic errors), it has repayment obligations; the product's audit trail can both protect and expose the customer. Build features that help customers quantify and refund.

### Gaps
- No DOJ case was found where an **independent AI coding vendor** (as opposed to a provider, plan, or plan-owned subsidiary like DxID) was a defendant for AI-generated codes; vendor FCA liability for AI coding is **unsettled**.
- No primary DOJ press release was retrieved for Practice Fusion/eClinicalWorks; figures are from secondary coverage.
- The OIG MA ICPG's exact wording on AI was not verified against the primary PDF.
- No sources found on actual market terms (indemnity caps, "audit guarantees," denial guarantees) used by AI coding vendors such as autonomous-coding companies; those terms are commercially confidential.
- The mechanics of the 60-day overpayment rule (42 CFR 401.305, revised in 2024-2025) were not researched in this pass.

## 4. Payer and policy backlash against AI-driven coding intensity; implications for product design

### Takeaway
2025-2026 saw open conflict: BCBSA published analyses (Mar and Sept 2026) blaming AI coding for ~$942M in added inpatient costs; Cigna (R49, Oct 2025) and Aetna began algorithmically downcoding level 4-5 office visits (prompting regulator pushback in CA and MD); CMS expanded Medicare Advantage RADV audits to all contracts; and DOJ is litigating "one-way" chart review. For an outpatient coding product, this means payers will scrutinize practices whose E/M distribution shifts after adoption, so the product must be bi-directional, documentation-grounded, and produce an audit trail that can defend each code.

### Cited Findings
- **BCBSA Sept 2026 analysis:** hospital coding changes added ~$942M to Blue plan costs (2023-2025), with ~$653M from more frequent billing of secondary conditions; ~70% of the intensity increase came from 55,000+ cases moving to higher-paying groups; share of complex inpatient cases rose from ~37% to ~40%; based on claims (not charts), released as a white paper, and did not prove AI caused each increase. — [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/healthcare/2026/ai-generated-medical-coding-adds-nearly-1-billion-to-blue-cross-costs/); [CNBC (Oct 1, 2026)](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/01/health-care-costs-insurance-claims-billing-ai.html); [WBAP](https://www.wbap.com/2026/09/28/blue-cross-says-ai-enabled-hospital-coding-added-942-million-in-costs/); [Becker's](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/bcbs-study-hospital-ai-billing-tools-may-be-driving-up-healthcare-costs-by-billions/)
- An earlier BCBSA study (Mar 2026) used commercial inpatient claims from plans covering ~62M members. — [STAT (Mar 9, 2026)](https://www.statnews.com/2026/03/09/bcbs-study-hospitals-use-ai-upcoding-drive-up-prices/); [Becker's Payer](https://www.beckerspayer.com/payer/bcbs-study-hospital-ai-billing-tools-may-be-driving-up-healthcare-costs-by-billions/)
- **Hospital rebuttal:** AHA says BCBSA did not review medical records, cites a ~5% rise in case-mix severity 2019-2024, and estimates inpatient coding intensity added only ~0.3 percentage points to commercial spending growth. — [AHA blog (Oct 5, 2026)](https://www.aha.org/news/blog/2026-10-05-better-information-better-care-how-coding-supports-todays-complex-patients)
- **Outpatient evidence:** Trilliant Health found a "consistent upward redistribution" of new and established outpatient E/M visits toward 99204-99205 and 99214-99215 at six large systems (2018-2024) following AI-enabled scribing adoption. — [Trilliant Health](https://www.trillianthealth.com/increased-outpatient-coding-intensity-following-hospital-adoption-of-ai-enabled-scribing)
- **Payer AI arms race:** STAT (June 2026) describes provider and insurer algorithms fighting over the same record; a PwC survey of 27 health plans found ~70% rank provider AI documentation/coding tools as a top-three cost inflator. — [STAT (June 3, 2026)](https://www.statnews.com/2026/06/03/ai-arms-race-medical-building-waste/)
- **Cigna R49 "E/M Coding Accuracy" (effective Oct 1, 2025):** may reduce 99204-99205, 99214-99215, 99244-99245 by one level when diagnosis and other claim-based criteria don't support the level; physicians with 5+ downcoded claims can request review; Cigna says ~1% of in-network physicians affected. — [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/fpm/blogs/getting-paid/cigna-downcoding-em); [ACDIS](https://acdis.org/articles/news-new-cigna-policy-automatically-%E2%80%98downcode%E2%80%99-certain-em-codes)
- Cigna paused R49 for California HMO plans pending DMHC review after a CMA inquiry. — [California Medical Association](https://www.cmadocs.org/newsroom/news/view/ArticleId/50993/Cigna-agrees-to-pause-controversial-downcoding-policy)
- Maryland Insurance Administration (Mar 13, 2026) fined Cigna $80,000, ordered it to stop automatic downcoding, and to reprocess claims back to Oct 1, 2025 (secondary source). — [WCHSB Insights (Mar 25, 2026)](https://insights.wchsb.com/2026/03/25/maryland-fines-cigna-80k-and-orders-it-to-stop-automatic-downcoding-what-every-provider-needs-to-know/)
- **Aetna Claim and Code Review Program:** targets level 4-5 E/M via vendor review; expanded from 12 pilot states to all commercial states except Louisiana, with MA expansion planned; specialty societies sent opposition letters (AAO-HNS, Sept 3, 2026, per a billing-firm post). — [ASIPP](https://asipp.org/important-news-aetna-and-cigna-announce-automatic-downcoding-policies/); [ENT Billing Services](https://www.entbillingservices.com/resources/weekly-news/aetna-cigna-ent-downcoding-em-2026); [LUGPA tracker](https://www.lugpa.org/insurer-downcoding-policies)
- **CMS RADV expansion (May 2025):** CMS plans to audit all eligible MA contracts annually (~550 vs ~60), samples of 35-200 enrollees, extrapolation of findings, and coder workforce growth from ~40 to ~2,000. — [Wolters Kluwer (Feb 2026)](https://www.wolterskluwer.com/en/expert-insights/cms-guidelines-for-radv-audits); [ChartRequest](https://chartrequest.com/radv-audits-2025)
- **One-way chart review litigation** (Anthem) and OIG Feb 2026 MA guidance on AI prompts - see Section 3. — [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2406/2026-01-13-medicare-advantage-under-microscope-enforcement); [Parker Poe](https://www.parkerpoe.com/news/2026/06/oig-guidance-warns-physician-practices-of-compliance-risks)
- UCHealth was detected because CMS data analytics flagged it as a "high outlier" for 99285. — [DOJ](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/uchealth-agrees-pay-23m-resolve-allegations-fraudulent-billing-emergency-department-visits)

### Inferences
- **Commercial payers now run outlier analytics on E/M distributions;** a customer whose level 4/5 share jumps after adopting the product will draw downcoding, prepayment review, or audits. The product should show customers their E/M distribution vs. specialty benchmarks before and after adoption and warn about outlier drift.
- **Design implications ("compliance by design"):**
  - *Bi-directional accuracy:* report and act on overcoding with the same priority as undercoding; publish both directions in customer dashboards and in marketing (avoid "revenue uplift" as the sole value metric).
  - *Evidence-linked suggestions:* every code change cites chart text and the specific guideline (e.g., 2021 AMA E/M MDM elements, ICD-10-CM guidelines) so a coder or auditor can verify it.
  - *Immutable audit trail:* who/what proposed each change, model version, prompt/rules version, evidence, human approver, timestamp; retain per customer record-retention needs (Medicare-related records are commonly retained 6-10 years; verify).
  - *No documentation fabrication:* never add diagnoses or text to the medical record; route documentation gaps to clinician queries that are non-leading and compliant (CDI query standards).
  - *Model governance:* version control, pre-deployment validation on held-out charts with certified-coder gold labels, ongoing accuracy monitoring by direction, drift alerts, and periodic external coding audits.
  - *Payer-policy awareness:* support payer-specific rules (e.g., Cigna/Aetna level 4-5 scrutiny) to help customers avoid denials, which is also a selling point.
- Payer backlash cuts both ways commercially: it increases demand for "audit-defensible" coding (accuracy and denial prevention) while reducing tolerance for "revenue maximization" positioning.

### Gaps
- BCBSA primary documents were not retrieved; figures come from press coverage. Most BCBSA evidence is inpatient, not outpatient.
- 2026 status of Cigna R49 beyond CA/MD actions is unclear.
- No CMS rule or guidance specifically regulating provider-side AI coding tools was found; the CMS Administrator remark that AI will be "inflationary" before it saves money (Sept 2026) appeared only in a search summary and was not verified.

## 5. State AI-in-healthcare laws and FDA status

### Takeaway
Current state AI healthcare laws (California AB 3030/SB 1120, Texas TRAIGA, Utah disclosure law, Colorado AI Act as amended) mostly target patient-facing communications, clinical decisions, utilization review by insurers, or "consequential decisions" about consumers; none found specifically regulates back-office coding AI, though Colorado's scope and HIPAA exemptions need counsel review before Jan 1, 2027. FDA: billing/claims software is excluded from the medical-device definition by statute (21st Century Cures Act), so a pure coding product is not a device; risk arises only if the product starts making clinical recommendations.

### Cited Findings
- **Colorado:** SB 26-189 (signed May 14, 2026) delays the Colorado AI Act from June 30, 2026 to Jan 1, 2027 and "significantly" scales it back: drops the duty of care, deployer risk-management programs, impact assessments, and certain AG reporting; developers must give deployers information on intended and potentially harmful uses, training data categories, and oversight instructions; individuals keep rights to access/correct data and "meaningful human review of adverse automated decisions." The original act covered consequential decisions in areas "such as employment, housing, health care and education." — [Hunton Andrews Kurth (May 22, 2026)](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-ai-act-amended-and-effective-date-delayed)
- A secondary tracker summary indicated HIPAA covered entities and BAs may be exempt from the main developer/deployer sections (except employment decisions) but still owe a general notice; this conflicted with other summaries and was not verified against bill text. — [Clunic Colorado AI Act page (secondary)](https://clunic.com/regulations/colorado-ai-act); [Glacis tracker](https://www.glacis.io/guide-state-ai-laws)
- **Texas TRAIGA** (signed June 2025, effective Jan 1, 2026): bans certain intentional harmful AI uses; for healthcare, practitioners must give patients conspicuous written disclosure of AI use in diagnosis or treatment; Texas also limits AI in medical-necessity determinations and requires "meaningful human oversight in clinical decision-making." — [Akerman (Jan 2026)](https://www.akerman.com/en/perspectives/hrx-new-year-new-ai-rules-healthcare-ai-laws-now-in-effect.html); [Morgan Lewis (Apr 2026)](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/04/ai-enforcement-accelerates-as-federal-policy-stalls-and-states-step-in)
- **California AB 3030** (effective Jan 2025): providers using generative AI to send patients communications about clinical information must include an AI disclaimer and how to reach a human (exempt if a licensed human reviews first). **SB 1120:** physician review required for utilization-review decisions (insurer side). — [Akerman](https://www.akerman.com/en/perspectives/hrx-new-year-new-ai-rules-healthcare-ai-laws-now-in-effect.html); [Greenberg Traurig 2026 outlook](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2025/12/2026-outlook-artificial-intelligence)
- **Utah** AI Policy Act (amended 2025 by SB 226): businesses must disclose generative AI use if a consumer clearly asks; licensed occupations must proactively disclose in "high-risk" interactions (e.g., collecting health data, personalized advice on significant decisions); SB 319 (AI disclosure in preauthorization) listed as effective Jan 1, 2027. — [Layer3Labs state tracker](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/state-ai-laws-by-state); [LiveCompliance AI healthcare regulations](https://livecompliance.com/learn/ai-healthcare-regulations/) (secondary trackers)
- State AGs are using UDAP (consumer-protection) statutes against AI marketing claims and data practices; AG investigations often precede class actions. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/04/ai-enforcement-accelerates-as-federal-policy-stalls-and-states-step-in)
- **FDA - statutory exclusion:** 21 U.S.C. 360j(o)(1)(A) excludes from the device definition software "for administrative support of a health care facility, including the processing and maintenance of financial records," claims and billing information, appointment schedules, business analytics, population health management, and similar functions. — [21 U.S.C. 360j (LII)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/360j)
- FDA's Clinical Decision Support guidance was reissued Jan 6, 2026 (re-issued Jan 29, 2026), replacing the 2022 version; it addresses 520(o)(1)(E) clinical decision support (four criteria) and is nonbinding; billing/scheduling software falls under the separate administrative-support exclusion. — [ABA Health Law (2026)](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/health_law/news/2026/fda-guidance-clinical-decision-support-software/); [CITI Program](https://about.citiprogram.org/blog/clinical-decision-support-compliance-fdas-2026-expectations/); [FDA guidance download](https://www.fda.gov/media/191560/download)

### Inferences
- **A pre-bill coding product is not an FDA medical device** under the administrative-support exclusion (confirmed by statute). Keep it that way: do not market or design features that recommend diagnoses for treatment purposes, triage patients, or tell clinicians what care to deliver. CDI-style queries asking a clinician to clarify documentation are administrative in purpose, but if a feature starts suggesting clinical diagnoses from labs/vitals for clinical action, the CDS criteria could come into play (gray area; not tested by FDA).
- **State AI laws mostly do not reach back-office coding**, because they regulate (a) patient-facing generative-AI communications (CA AB 3030), (b) clinical diagnosis/treatment disclosures (TX), (c) insurer utilization review (CA SB 1120, TX), or (d) consequential consumer decisions (CO). Pre-bill coding affects the provider's claim, not a decision about the patient's care or eligibility. Possible edges: AI-generated patient billing statements or patient-facing explanations (AB 3030-style disclosure), and Colorado if billing outcomes are construed as "consequential decisions" about health care financial services (unsettled; obtain counsel opinion before Jan 1, 2027).
- **Marketing claims are a live risk:** the Pieces Technologies Texas AG settlement shows accuracy/performance claims about health AI can trigger consumer-protection enforcement; substantiate any accuracy claim (methodology, sample, gold standard).

### Gaps
- Colorado SB 26-189 bill text (definition of "consequential decision," HIPAA exemption scope) not read directly.
- Texas SB 1188 (2025, EHR/AI provisions) and other 2026 state bills (e.g., Illinois, New York) not researched.
- No state law found that specifically governs AI medical coding or billing software.

## 6. ONC HTI-1 decision support intervention (DSI) transparency rules: do they apply to third-party coding tools?

### Takeaway
No, not directly. HTI-1's DSI "source attribute" (model-card-like) requirements bind certified health IT developers (EHR vendors) for DSIs they supply in their certified modules; third-party tools are outside that obligation, though EHRs must let customers record attributes for them. Moreover, the HTI-5 proposed rule (Dec 2025/Jan 2026) would eliminate the source-attribute and predictive-DSI risk-management requirements; final status unknown as of Oct 2026.

### Cited Findings
- HTI-1 final rule published Jan 9, 2024 (Federal Register), establishing the DSI certification criterion at 45 CFR 170.315(b)(11). — [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/01/09/2023-28857/health-data-technology-and-interoperability-certification-program-updates-algorithm-transparency-and); [ONC test method](https://healthit.gov/test-method/decision-support-interventions)
- Source attribute requirements cover only DSIs supplied by the certified developer; developers are not required to obtain source attribute information for another party's Predictive DSI unless they supply it as part of their Health IT Module; health systems adding their own or third-party predictive tools can use the EHR's functionality to record source attributes. Evidence-based DSIs carry 13 source attributes, Predictive DSIs 31. — [ONC DSI Criterion Resource Guide (May 2024)](https://healthit.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/DSI-Criterion-Resource-Guide_508.pdf); [ONC HTI-1 DSI presentation](https://healthit.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/DSI_HTI1-Final-Rule-Presentation_508.pdf); [Mintz (Jan 2024)](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2146/2024-01-08-hhs-onc-hti-1-final-rule-introduces-new-transparency)
- **HTI-5 proposed rule:** would eliminate both the developer requirement to supply source attribute information and the requirement for risk-management practices for predictive DSIs, scale back 170.315(b)(11), retire 170.315(a)(9), and eliminate 34 / revise 7 of 60 certification criteria; DSI revision effective on the final rule's effective date. — [Covington (Jan 15, 2026)](https://www.covingtonblogs.com/2026/01/15/hhs-proposes-changes-to-the-health-it-certification-program-and-information-blocking-regulations-in-hti-5-proposed-rule/); [ASTP/ONC HTI-5 information session slides](https://healthit.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/2026AnnualMeeting_HTI-5-Proposed-Rule-Information-Session.pdf); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/health-industry-washington-watch/102m1ob/hti-5-proposal-signals-health-it-deregulation-and-fhir-first-interoperability-and/)

### Inferences
- A standalone pre-bill coding product that is not part of a certified EHR module has **no HTI-1 obligation**. If the product is ever embedded/resold by an EHR vendor as part of its certified module, the EHR vendor (not the startup) would bear DSI obligations, and would likely push model-card information requirements down by contract (moot if HTI-5 is finalized as proposed).
- Regardless of HTI-1, health-system AI governance committees increasingly ask for model-card-style documentation (intended use, training data, validation, known limitations, monitoring); preparing a one-to-two-page "model fact sheet" is cheap and speeds procurement.
- Separately, ONC information-blocking rules primarily affect EHR vendors, HIEs, and providers; a startup that only consumes data via EHR APIs is generally on the receiving end (useful leverage when EHR vendors restrict access). Not researched in depth.

### Gaps
- Whether HTI-5 was finalized by Oct 2026 was not confirmed.
- Whether a billing-coding tool would even meet the HTI-1 "DSI" definition (which is about supporting clinical decision-making) was not confirmed in primary text.

## 7. Practical compliance roadmap with rough costs and timelines

### Takeaway
Before a first pilot with real PHI (~1-3 months, ~$15K-$45K cash plus founder time): BAA templates, HIPAA policies and risk analysis, BAA-covered cloud/LLM stack, a pen test, basic insurance, and a coding-compliance policy (bi-directional, human-approved, evidence-linked). Before scaling to health systems (~6-18 months, ~$40K-$100K/yr ongoing, more with HITRUST): SOC 2 Type II, $5M+ Tech E&O/cyber, formal compliance program per OIG GCPG elements (compliance officer, external coding audits), and model governance documentation; HITRUST i1/r2 when health systems or payers require it.

### Inferences (proposed roadmap; costs are synthesized estimates from the sources above plus judgment)

**Phase 0 - Before touching any real PHI (weeks 0-6)**
| Item | Why | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|
| BAA template (customer-facing) + subcontractor BAA inventory | Required by HIPAA once PHI flows | $2K-$8K healthcare counsel (or adapt customer paper) |
| Cloud under BAA (AWS/Azure/GCP), LLM via BAA-covered, zero-retention configuration; PHI-scrubbed logging | Subcontractor BAs; minimum necessary | Mostly engineering time; BAA itself free with enterprise cloud accounts |
| HIPAA policies, risk analysis, Privacy/Security officer designation, workforce training | Security Rule basics; buyers ask for evidence | Included in compliance platform ($7.5K-$15K/yr) or ~$5K-$15K consultant |
| Coding-compliance policy: suggest-mode default, bi-directional findings, evidence citation, no chart amendments, certified-coder QA sample | FCA/OIG risk controls (UCHealth, Anthem, OIG 1998 guidance) | Founder/clinical-advisor time; a certified coder advisor (part-time) |
| Pricing review (avoid pure % of incremental revenue) | OIG percentage-billing concern; DxID | 1-2 hours healthcare regulatory counsel ($1K-$3K) |
| Basic insurance: Tech E&O + cyber ($1M-$2M), GL, D&O | Pilot contracts will ask | Likely low-to-mid thousands/yr at seed stage (no health-specific source) |

**Phase 1 - First paid pilots with outpatient groups (months 1-6)**
- Third-party pen test + remediation letter ($5K-$30K).
- Start SOC 2 (platform + Type I, then begin Type II observation window); Type I report achievable in ~1-3 months; Type II after a 3-6+ month window.
- Security questionnaire library (standard answers, data-flow diagram, subprocessor list incl. LLM provider).
- Audit trail and model versioning in product; accuracy validation report against certified-coder gold labels (both directions).
- Contract terms: liability cap (e.g., 12 months' fees) with data-breach super-cap; clear statement that the customer retains final coding responsibility; audit-support commitment (vendor provides rationale/evidence for payer audits); no FCA penalty indemnity.

**Phase 2 - Scaling to health systems / large MSOs (months 6-18)**
- SOC 2 Type II report (annual), ideally with HIPAA mapping.
- Increase Tech E&O + cyber to $5M (sometimes $10M) combined to meet hospital contract minimums.
- Formal compliance program per OIG GCPG: compliance officer (fractional acceptable early), compliance committee, annual risk assessment, hotline, external annual coding-accuracy audit by an independent certified-coder firm, overpayment-reporting workflow support for customers.
- Model-governance documentation ("model fact sheet": intended use, data, validation, monitoring) for AI governance committees.
- Monitor customers' E/M distributions for outlier drift (payer downcoding and audit exposure).

**Phase 3 - Enterprise/payer-grade (18+ months, if required)**
- HITRUST i1 (~$70K-$200K, 5-12 months) or r2 (~$100K-$500K+, 8-18 months) when large health systems or payers mandate it.
- Counsel review of Colorado AI Act applicability (effective Jan 1, 2027) and any new state laws; monitor HIPAA Security Rule final (target July 2027) and HTI-5.

**Rough budget summary (synthesized):** pre-pilot cash ~$15K-$45K; year 1 total compliance/security/insurance ~$40K-$100K (SOC 2 + pen test + counsel + insurance + coder audits); HITRUST adds $70K-$500K depending on level.
